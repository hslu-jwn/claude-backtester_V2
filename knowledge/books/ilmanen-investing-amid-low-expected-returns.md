# Part 1: Part I and ch.4: investment winter, allocation history, asset-class returns


### Overview
- Covers the Foreword, Ch.1-3 (Part I: the "low expected return" challenge, investor types, history of institutional allocation) and Ch.4 (liquid asset class premia: cash, equity, bond, credit, commodity). Ch.5 starts at the end of the range and is not covered; the style premia (value, momentum, carry, defensive, trend, seasonals) are in Ch.6 and are NOT in this range. Only a one-figure preview exists here (Fig. 1.2).
- Stance: an AQR author, systematic, diversification-first, humble on forecasts. Core claim: starting yields and valuations are at record lows, so 2020s expected returns are low. Past realized returns were inflated by "windfall gains" from falling discount rates. Do not extrapolate the rearview mirror.
- Method value for us: the discount-rate-effect decomposition (how much of a sample's return came from re-pricing, not from a repeatable premium). This is a direct template for an "honest backtest" check on any sample that ran through a valuation trend.
- Little here is tradable on our data at M1/daily frequency. The usable parts are (a) methods that make tests more honest, (b) long-history base rates for asset-class premia (term premium, credit, commodity), (c) a few mechanism items (fallen-angel forced selling, commodity roll, diversification/rebalancing return).
- Ch.4 is mostly updating the 2011 book: mean sizes are given, with few new trading rules.

### Chapter notes

### Foreword (Asness) (pp. xiii-xv)
- Ten more years of data plus back-extended histories (credit, commodity, style premia up to ~100 years) were "out of sample" if nobody peeked. They confirmed the premia as real.
- Warning: valuation change inside a sample distorts the estimated natural return. A strategy that starts expensive and ends more expensive (value spread, passive equity) looks better than it is, even over 70 years. Estimate returns without giving credit for re-pricing.
- "Time dilation": a 3-5 year painful period looks bearable on a backtest chart and is hard to live through. Best strategy is the one you can hold.
- Contrarian valuation-based market timing did not work in a decade of ever-richer stocks. Trading costs matter more when expected returns are low.

### Ch.1 - Introduction (pp. 3-13)
- Fig 1.1 (p.7): yield-based expected real return of US equities and 10Y Treasuries since 1900. Sept 2021 real yields 2.9% (stocks) and -0.9% (bonds) are at the 2nd and 1st percentile of 122 years. Equity real yield = average of D/P and half of CAEY, plus 1.5% growth, no mean reversion.
- Fig 1.2 (p.9), 1926-2020, before costs and fees: global equity premium 6.2% p.a.; term, credit and commodity premia 1.8-3.5%. Five style premia (value, momentum, carry, defensive, trend) scaled to 5% vol. Sharpe ratios range from 0.53 (value) to 0.89 (trend). Style SRs are gross of costs and fees, and the high SR partly reflects diversification. Net of costs and now that the ideas are known ("alpha morphed into alternative beta") they would be lower.
- Value's recent bad years made many investors give up on style premia (details in Ch.6, not in this range).
- Luck dominates skill over months and years. Judge process, not outcome (outcome bias). Decades are needed to separate luck from skill. Equity markets have losing decades (2000-09).
- "Base-rate" mindset: long-run evidence of nine premia is the prior, and a specific view must overcome it.
- Beliefs: prefer diversification, systematic over discretionary, strategic over tactical timing, probabilistic over story.

### Ch.2 - The Secular Low Expected Return Challenge (pp. 15-26)
- Discount rate effect (Fig 2.1, p.18, footnotes 2-3 give the method). Regress monthly excess returns on contemporaneous yield change. The slope is the empirical duration. Windfall gain = slope x net yield change, spread over the sample. The regression intercept is a better estimate of the ex-ante premium than the realized mean.
  - 10Y Treasury 1981-2020: realized excess return 3.3% p.a.; windfall 1.5% (yield fell 12.4% to 0.9%, empirical duration 5.3, correlation about -0.95); neutral excess 1.8%.
  - S&P500 1981-2020: realized excess 7.8%; windfall 2.7% (CAEY fell 10.3% to 2.9%, slope -14.6, correlation about -0.9); neutral excess 5.1%. So more than one third of the equity premium in the sample was repricing.
  - Result is robust to raw vs log returns and monthly vs annual changes.
  - Footnote: for many long-short factors the link between valuation change and return is weak, so estimate it empirically instead of assuming 1:1.
- Yield-based 60/40 expected real return (Fig 2.2) is below 2% in 2021 vs a 5% historical norm. It has imperfect but real predictive power for the next 10Y real return.
- Surveys (SPF, CFOs, capital-market-assumption providers): expected nominal equity return halved (10% to 5%) 1992-2021; expected equity premium over cash stayed stable. The decline in expected cash rates drove the richening.
- Low cash rate means expected equity premium over cash is above average even though total return is low.
- Long history (Schmelzing, Fig 2.4): real yields drifted from double digits (1300s) to about 3% (1900). Real growth was near 0% for centuries, but growth was fast in centuries of low real yields. Disinflation 1982+; the 2020s inflation risks are mentioned.
- Decadal table 2.1: after a double-digit-excess decade for S&P (20s, 50s, 90s) usually comes a weak one; but the contrarian pattern is weak. 2010s: everything except US large-cap/tech, including diversifiers and alternative risk premia, hurt. Easy central banks cut macro trends short and hurt trend followers and macro traders.
- "Slow pain" (low coupons for decades) vs "fast pain" (repricing losses).

### Ch.3 - Investor Types and Their Responses (pp. 27-47)
- Mostly institutional context: DB pensions, DC savers, endowments; funding ratios; glide paths; history of asset allocation (stocks over bonds after WWII; rise of alternatives); ownership shift to funds/ETFs; active-to-passive flows after 2006.
- Numbers possibly useful as context: US corporate DB funding ratio fell from 105% to 79% in 2008. A DC saver aiming at 75% replacement needs about 8% of salary at 5.5% real, but about 15% at 3.5% real (Fig 3.9, Ilmanen-Rauseo-Truax).
- US public pension assumed returns (about 7%) sit far above yield-based 60/40 expected returns (Fig 3.10). Reach-for-yield (more equity, illiquids, factor tilts, leverage) is the response. All investors cannot raise equity risk at once.
- Fig 3.5: alternatives share of pensions rose to about 20-25%. Global market portfolio box 3.1: about $106trn equities, $120trn bonds, private assets under $10trn.
- Relevance to us: forced-flow actors (liability-driven investors de-risking by funding ratio, target-date glide paths, index funds) are potential "must-trade" counterparties. The book only describes them; no flow data or test is given.

### Ch.4 - Liquid Asset Class Premia (pp. 51-87)
**4.1 Cash (pp. 53-56).** Real cash rate is near 0% globally and about +1% in the US since 1900. Use survey-based inflation expectations (Livingston) to build the expected real rate. Negative real rates since 2008 are exceptional. A currency-hedged investor earns his own cash rate plus the foreign asset's excess return, and total return cannot be levered, only excess return.
**4.2 Equity premium (pp. 57-70).**
- DMS 1900-2020 (21 countries, full history): global compound 8.3% (9.7% arithmetic), real 5.3% (6.7%), premium over bills 4.4% (5.9%), over bonds 3.1% (4.3%), SR 0.35. US SR 0.40, t about 4.4 over 121 years (t = SR x sqrt(years)). World ex-US 0.29, EM 0.25.
- US real 6.6% vs non-US 4.5% over 1900-2020; 11.3% vs 4.4% in 2010-20. The US edge comes mostly from faster dividend growth (1.9% vs about 0 elsewhere), not from valuation changes.
- Decade premia (Fig 4.5): 1950s best; 1910s worst; EM -12% in the 1940s. Max drawdown of S&P excess return 84% (1929-32); longest underwater 1968-86 and 1929-45. Real-return underwater periods: US 16 years at longest, Germany 55, Japan 51, UK and World 22. US equity-minus-bond premium had a 20-year negative stretch ending March 2020. McQuarrie finds no equity-bond premium 1793-1921.
- Variance drain approximately 0.5 x vol^2 (16% vol gives 1.3%).
- Forward model: E(R) = DY + g + E(V), with g=1.5% real EPS growth (century of data). Mid-2021: 1.7% income + 1.5% growth gives 3.2% real, 5.5% nominal, arithmetic 6.8%, SR 0.43.
- Refinements: use net payout yield (dividends + buybacks - issuance) instead of dividend yield (dividend yield 5.0% / 3.6% / 1.9% and net total yield 3.7% / 2.1% / 1.5% across 1901-71 / 1972-99 / 2000-20); use per-share vs aggregate growth carefully (dilution of about 2% per year in 20th century US, now about zero or accretive since ca. 2006). L'Her et al. 43-country 1997-2017: market return has 0.0 correlation with GDP growth but 0.60 with net buybacks. China: huge dilution explains low returns despite fast growth (Box 4.2).
- Mean reversion in valuation is slow and weak; CAPE 37 (mid-2021) vs 27 recent mean vs 17 long-run. The author chooses not to assume reversion but notes the bearish alternative (GMO, RAFI).
- Valuation (CAEY) outside the US is higher-yielding; US-vs-ROW valuation gap is at a record.
**4.3 Bond risk premium (pp. 70-75).**
- Realized global term premium 1920-2020: about 1.8% p.a., vs ex-ante term spread average 1.4%. Bond SR about 0.3 (10Y Treasury), 0.4 (global composite). Little reward beyond 10Y maturity; SRs are higher for short maturities (leverage aversion).
- Survey-based decomposition of the 10Y yield (Fig 4.12): expected inflation, expected real short rate, bond risk premium. All three fell. BRP went from 3-4% to below zero. Survey BRP predicts worse than the simple term spread for near-term returns (forecast errors contribute).
- Why BRP fell: lower inflation risk premium, safe-haven role (stock-bond correlation negative since about 2000), QE and pension demand. Stock-bond correlation sign depends on relative size of growth vs inflation uncertainty.
- Consensus and forwards predicted rising yields for ~30 years and were wrong ("widowmaker" short JGB). Starting yield predicts bond portfolio return best at horizons near twice duration (Leibowitz et al.). A steep curve is a good but unreliable predictor of excess bond return.
**4.4 Credit (pp. 75-82).**
- IG credit premium over duration-matched Treasuries: older 0.3-0.4%; new long-history estimate (Asvanunt-Richardson, 1936-2014) about 1.5%, 1.0% from 1973, 0.7% from 1989, but early-data quality is questioned (Kizer et al., McQuarrie). Credit portfolio beat equity+Treasury mix by 1.5% (IG) and 3% (HY) 1993-2019 (Ben Dor et al.), explained by short-vol exposure and leverage aversion, not illiquidity.
- Spread capture: IG earned 75bp vs 132bp average OAS (56%); HY 2.8% vs 5.2% (54%). Break-even default loss: 15-25bp IG, 200-250bp HY. The IG leakage is blamed on index investors' fire sales of fallen angels.
- Fallen angels (just downgraded to HY, forced selling by IG-only holders): persisted after being publicised. 1997-2020 4.6% vs 2.3% for all-HY excess return; 2010-2020 6.5% vs 4.2%. Vol 11% vs 10%. (Our data have no bond-level credit, so not testable.)
- Front-end credit: information ratio falls with maturity (0.45 for 1-3Y vs 0.05 for 25Y+, 1989-2020; 0.74 vs 0.12 since 2010). Vulnerable in crises, and depends on financing spread.
- Active fixed-income managers outperform mainly by holding persistent positive credit beta (and gross-of-fee, survivorship), not style premia; only carry exposure is clearly positive.
**4.5 Commodities (pp. 82-87).**
- Equal-weighted composite of up to 29 futures, 1877-2020: excess return GM 3.4%, AM 4.9%, vol 18%, SR 0.27. Single commodities: GM 0.0%, AM 4.0%, vol 28%, SR 0.12 ("turning water into wine": diversification/rebalancing return). 1900-2020 GM 4.9%, SR 0.37. Bhardwaj et al. 1871-2018: GM 4.3%, all sectors positive; DJCI since 1933 3.7%.
- Most of the long-run return comes from spot return; roll return is persistently negative in recent decades (contango). Non-surviving commodities are included.
- Carry (roll) and momentum patterns weakened in the 2010s. Index-level carry/momentum timing worked only at short horizons, no multi-year predictability, no value signal. Best forecast = constant premium of about 3% over cash for the diversified basket.
- Gold: roughly zero real return over centuries; real price inversely related to real rates but weakly; ETF demand matters. Inflation sensitivity (Fig 4.17): commodities, gold and break-evens are the only assets benefiting from inflation surprises; stocks, bonds, real estate are hurt. Oil: cost-push inflation; industrial metals: demand-pull; precious metals: loss of central bank credibility.

- **portfolio/diversification:** diversification return in commodities; bonds at equity vol have century performance similar to equities (Fig 4.11); credit adds beyond equity+Treasury mix; 2010s hurt every diversifier versus US 60/40.

### Testable ideas
(Range is thin on tradable rules. Ideas below are mostly method checks or long-horizon premia that could be proxied in our data.)

1. ★ **Windfall-gain decomposition for every sample we test** - For a candidate strategy or asset leg, regress monthly returns on contemporaneous change in the relevant yield/valuation variable (e.g. FRED 10Y yield change for the Treasury month-end duration stream; CAEY or P/E change for share sorts). Windfall = slope x net change; report the intercept-based premium next to the raw mean. Pre-register: pass only if the intercept premium keeps t >= 3. Source: Ch.2, Fig 2.1 and footnotes 2-3 (pp. 18-19). Author evidence: bond 3.3% realized vs 1.8% neutral; equity 7.8% vs 5.1%. Fit: good (FRED yields, Yahoo 10Y futures, Alpaca share prices; no cost issue because it is a diagnostic). Differs: not a strategy but an honesty filter; directly tests whether our passed Treasury month-end stream (2015+ sample, yields roughly flat to up, but earlier sample falling) lives on repricing.
2. ★ **Treasury term premium and carry check for the month-end duration stream** - Compare the stream's long-10Y-futures return on the last 3 days of the month with unconditional 10Y excess return (carry plus drift) per day, so the "must-buy" effect is separated from the base term premium (about 1.8% p.a., about 0.007% per day) and from yield-fall windfall (Ch.4.3, p.70). Author claim: term premium 1.8% p.a. realized vs 1.4% ex ante, SR about 0.3. Fit: good; uses FRED and Yahoo data. Differs: sanity baseline for the one passed idea, not a new strategy.
3. ★ **Diversification/rebalancing return across crypto perps or CFD baskets** - Equal-weight rebalanced basket of high-vol instruments earns a return above the average single-asset geometric return (commodities: single GM 0.0% vs basket GM 3.4% with the same 4-5% AM). Rule: hold an equal-risk or equal-weight basket of the ~34 FTMO CFDs (or the crypto perps), rebalance monthly, compare to the average of the single-asset buy-and-holds. Source: Table 4.4, Ch.4.5 (p.83). Fit: poor to moderate. It is a long-only beta effect with swap/funding costs on CFDs, tiny net, no counterparty who must trade, and not obviously a premium; useful mainly as a decomposition tool for the crypto taker-flow sort (how much of the long-short return is rebalancing). Differs: not directional, not pattern-based.
4. **Commodity-futures carry and momentum at index and cross-section level on EIA energy contracts 1-4** - Roll yield (contract 1 vs 2) signal: long where the curve is backwardated. Author states cross-section and index-level carry and momentum worked until 2010 but weakened in the 2010s and are weak beyond short horizons. Source: Ch.4.5 (p.84). Fit: moderate; EIA contracts 1-4 exist (crude, products, gas) and futures costs are low, but we only have the energy complex, so the cross-section is tiny (about 4-6 series). Differs: GSCI roll test is spent; this is a signal-based roll rather than the calendar roll. Expect a decayed result, as the author says.
5. **Fallen-angel forced selling (analogue on shares)** - Event study of forced sellers: stocks removed from an index or downgraded below a mandate threshold. Bond version: IG-only holders must sell within weeks of downgrade; fallen angels earn 4.6% vs 2.3% excess return for all HY (1997-2020), edge unchanged after publicity. Source: Ch.4.4 (pp. 80-81). Fit: poor as stated (no bond-level data). A share analogue needs index-membership or rating event dates, which we do not have, apart from Russell/S&P rebalancing that can be proxied from market cap rank in Alpaca data. Differs: genuine mechanism-based forced flow, but not in our failed price-pattern families.
6. **Front-end credit/low-risk leverage aversion (long short-duration, levered)** - Exploits that SR falls with maturity (IR 0.45 at 1-3Y to 0.05 at 25Y+). Fit: poor, we have no credit data; on Treasuries, check whether 2Y or 5Y futures levered to 10Y vol have higher SR than 10Y futures. Needs a Yahoo continuous 2Y/5Y series (we have only 10Y). Source: Ch.4.3 footnote 26 and 4.4 (p.71, 81). Differs: it is a duration-curve carry sort, not a price pattern; FRED yields can proxy it as a constant-maturity return estimate.
7. **Yield-curve steepness as predictor of 10Y Treasury excess return (monthly timing)** - Rule: scale duration exposure by term spread (10Y minus 3M) z-score. Author: steep curve predicts high excess returns, "not very reliable but as good as it gets." Source: Ch.4.3 (p.75). Fit: moderate (FRED data); risk of overfitting with few independent observations (the author warns about multiyear overlaps). Differs: macro timing, not a price pattern. Probably weak after costs; test as a level filter on the month-end stream rather than standalone.
8. **Equity-premium base-rate as shrinkage prior** - Use the long-run Sharpe base rates in Fig 1.2 and DMS (equity SR 0.35-0.40, term 0.3, commodity 0.27-0.37, credit about 0.3-0.5 for the style composite) as priors for shrunk forecasts: shrink estimated dev-sample SR toward the base rate with weights by t-statistic. Source: Ch.1 and Ch.4. Fit: good (method). Differs: this is part of our "shrunk forecasts" rule; the book provides concrete prior values.

### Warnings / pitfalls
- Realized returns of 1981-2020 (and any strongly trending valuation sample) contain big windfall gains. A third of equity premium and nearly half of the bond premium in that sample were repricing; do not extrapolate. Today's starting yields are lower too (double counting of error).
- Rearview-mirror expectations: after repricing gains, subjective expectations rise just when objective expectations fall.
- Even a decade is a short period; luck dominates months and years; need decades to separate luck from skill. Decadal contrarian patterns are weak.
- Style premia figures are before costs and fees, and publicity ("alpha morphing into beta") may reduce them; value, momentum and carry-style premia had a poor 2010s; commodity carry and momentum weakened too.
- Valuation-based timing: mean reversion is slow and structurally unstable; CAPE can stay high. Too few independent multi-year observations.
- Data quality of early history (corporate bonds before 1973, commodity series, 1800s US stock data) can bias premia up or down; credit premium estimate depends on the start date.
- Survivorship, gross-of-fee reporting and persistent beta tilts inflate active manager "alpha".
- Single instruments lose (variance drain) while diversified baskets win; judging a premium on one instrument understates it.
- Stock-bond correlation is regime-dependent (negative since about 2000, depends on inflation uncertainty); safe-haven bond returns can vanish if inflation returns.
- Short-maturity or low-risk tilt needs leverage and cheap financing, and fails in crisis (bonds trade on price).
- Forecast consensus on yields was persistently wrong for 30 years, a reminder that "must revert" arguments can be costly.

# Part 2: ch.5-7: illiquidity premium, style premia, alpha


### Overview
- Covers ch.5 Illiquidity Premia (private assets, less liquid public assets, liquidity provision, Box 5.2 calendar effects), ch.6 Style Premia (value, momentum/trend, carry, defensive/quality; Box 6.1 size) and ch.7 Alpha and Its Cousins (active vs passive, manager skill, demystifying hedge funds and superstars). Text stops at the start of ch.8.
- Note: the assigned range contains no separate "alternative premia" chapter. Style premia is ch.6, illiquidity is ch.5.
- Stance: all long-run premia are real but smaller and noisier than marketing says. Most numbers are AQR data, gross of trading costs (explicitly stated). The author is sceptical of "illiquidity premia" and of manager alpha, and keen on diversified style premia (value, momentum/trend, carry, defensive) that are pervasive (many asset classes), persistent (long history, out of sample) and robust (many specifications).
- Post-2009 news: value had a huge drawdown 2018-2020 (US B/P value -63% from 2007 to 2020), trend was flat in the 2010s (curtailed trends, central banks), momentum crashed in 2009 and Nov 2020, defensive/low-beta kept working in the 2010s, currency carry weakened in the zero-rate world, short-term reversal is gone (arbitraged by the mid-2000s), private equity edge has shrunk since 2006 vintages.
- Strong theme: overfitting vs crowding trade-off. Proprietary new signals look good because short histories and many degrees of freedom allow overfitting. Known factors risk overcrowding. Costs matter most for high-turnover strategies.

### Chapter notes

### Ch 5 - Illiquidity Premia (pp. 87-103)
- Liquidity has three facets (tightness, depth, resilience). Ranking: cash, futures, bonds, stocks, hedge funds, private assets. Illiquidity premia are never harvested alone, they come bundled with beta, size, credit and other premia.
- Size of private markets (Preqin mid-2020): $7.3trn total investable, about $4.5trn private equity, $1.1trn real estate, $0.9trn each infrastructure and private debt. At most 5% of public equity and 1% of public debt. Table 5.1 private returns are IRR-based and smooth, so risk looks too low.
- Housing vs equities (Jorda et al. 2019, 16 countries, 1870-2015): housing real return about equal to equities (7.26% vs 6.67% equal-weighted, equities ahead GDP-weighted). The author's check, US 1960-2020: equities ahead by 2.0% (arithmetic) or 0.7% (geometric). Housing return is almost all income, real capital gains tiny.
- Criticisms: net rental yield costs (30-40% of gross rent) extrapolated backwards, weak quality adjustment. UK endowment data show 1.6% lower return. Quality-adjusted real house price growth is estimated at -2.1% (Dimson-Marsh-Staunton). Haircut the Jorda estimates by about 1.5%.
- Commercial real estate NCREIF 1978-2020 decomposition (Pagliari method): gross yield 7.6%, net 5.1%, real rent growth -0.8%, cap rate fall (8% to 4%) a windfall; realized real return 5.4%. Forward: gross yield about 4%, net 2.7%, so expected real return about 2.7% or lower.
- Direct vs listed: REITs beat NCREIF (7% vs 4% over cash, 1978-2020), leverage-adjusted still no illiquidity premium (Ang-Nabar-Wald 2013). So no sign of an illiquidity premium in real estate.
- Buyout PE: 2-3% a year over S&P500 for 30+ years after about 5-6% all-in fees (CEM 5.7%), but vintages since 2006 show no outperformance by PME; against small-cap value or levered equity the edge vanishes. Valuation gap PE vs public narrowed (EBITDA/EV chart). Author notes the verdict has seesawed several times (Kaplan-Schoar, Stucke, Harris-Jenkinson-Kaplan, Phalippou, Brown-Kaplan).
- Key idea: smoothing service. Investors like smooth marked-to-model returns and pay for it by accepting a lower premium, so the fair illiquidity premium (3-5% for a decade lock-up, in normative models) is offset. Cambridge buyout drawdown in the GFC -25% vs small-cap value -60%. True PE vol about 20% not reported 9%, beta about 1.3 not 0.5 ("slow beta": smoothing helps in V-shaped, not in protracted bear markets).
- Less liquid public assets: small-cap premium beyond beta is mostly micro-caps. Amihud and Pastor-Stambaugh liquidity factors replicate worst (Hou et al. 2020) and are not tradable net of costs (Pastor-Stambaugh correlates with short-term reversal). Corporate bonds: after controlling for credit quality, less liquid bonds do not earn more (Richardson-Palhares 2019). Linkers show a premium mainly in flight-to-quality episodes.
- Liquidity provision (section 5.3), the most promising part on paper: short-term reversal, merger arbitrage, convertible arbitrage, fixed-income arbitrage (off-the-run vs on-the-run), event regularities (index additions and deletions S&P500/Russell/MSCI, government bond auctions, fallen angels, GSCI roll), long back-month vs short front-month futures. Capacity constrained, only intermediaries or low-cost traders make net profits.
- Box 5.2 calendar effects (brief, "costs make most unimplementable"): January and Halloween effects (2011 book). Same-month seasonality (Heston-Sadka 2008; Keloharju et al. 2016 find it stronger in factors and other asset classes). Announcement days: stocks, style premia and beta are rewarded on earnings and macro announcement days, but recent studies say most of the reward is earned hours before. Turn-of-month: selling pressure before month end ("dash for cash"), buying after new cash arrives (Etula et al. 2020). Within-day: much of equity premium and all of momentum profit is earned overnight (Lou-Polk-Skouros 2019); overnight returns predict next overnight positively and next intraday negatively. FOMC days and the period before them were bullish for equities (possible leakage).

### Ch 6 - Style Premia (pp. 105-137)
- Four styles: value, momentum (incl. trend), carry, defensive/quality. Test for inclusion: persistent, pervasive, robust. Author admits the choice has some hindsight. Correlation across asset classes and styles low, within-asset-class across regions 0.6+.
- Box 6.1 size: no size premium in long-run data beyond beta (Alquist-Israel-Moskowitz 2018; Asness et al. 2018). Residual is in untradeable micro-caps, and excluding small junk or small growth stocks revives it. Other premia are bigger in small caps on paper, but costs and capacity offset.
- Value (6.1): the Fama-French HML recipe (30/70 sort, annual rebalance, lagged book values). Two centuries of US stock selection value (Samonov). Table 6.2 Sharpe ratios 1984-Jun 2021: B/P raw US large 0.15, small 0.16, ex-US 0.35/0.30; multi-metric industry-adjusted US large 0.50, small 0.79, ex-US 0.73/0.99. Rules: multi-metric composite beats B/P alone, industry-neutral beats raw (the more robust finding). Value works somewhat better ex-US and in small caps. Country SRs 0.1-0.7 large caps.
- Value in other assets: equity indices (aggregate B/P or CAEY), bonds (real yield), currencies (PPP), commodities (5-year-ago price vs now, long-run mean reversion). Century of value Sharpe ratios by decade was mostly positive; negative in 1920s, 1950s, 2010s in several asset classes. Short-term reversal (STREV) had a paper SR 0.77 since the 1930s but was arbitraged away by the mid-2000s. LTREV SR 0.27 vs B/P 0.34, correlation 0.6.
- Value 2018-2020 post-mortem: not just FAMAG, not US-only, not B/P only, not explained by bond yields or crowding (no overweight in holdings), intangibles adjustment would not have saved it. Author: another bubble, spreads at record highs, spread wider ex-US. Contrarian strategies are "short structural change". Value drawdowns: -63% (2007-2020), -59% (1890-1904), -53% (1926-32), -35% (1998-2000). Value timing by own spread has limited usefulness (Asness et al. 2017).
- Value and momentum correlation near -0.5, so about 50/50 blend. Sweet spot: cheap and recently improving.
- Momentum/trend (6.2): stock momentum = past 12-month return skipping the last month; trend = 12-month lookback with one-month execution lag (author's deliberately conservative "poor man's cost adjustment"), vol-targeted, equal vol, no correlations. Multi-asset trend composite SR 0.89 (1926-2020, 12m with lag). Footnote: adding correlation 0.96; no lag 1.27; average of 1/3/12-month windows 1.67 (Hurst-Ooi-Pedersen); raw SRs 1m/3m/12m = 1.45/1.24/1.27, but with the one-month lag or estimated trading costs the fast 1-month trend has the LOWEST SR. Class SRs 0.44 (currencies) to 0.62 (bonds). Positive in virtually all 60+ assets.
- Trend works with less liquid markets (Babu et al. 2018: EM equities/currencies, swaps, CDS indices, exotic commodities, VIX futures, factor momentum). Factor momentum exists (Gupta-Kelly, Ehsani-Linnainmaa).
- Risks: stock momentum crashes after sharp turns (1932, spring 2009, Nov 2020); trend has "smile" payoff on equity returns (like a long straddle), loses in choppy markets; trend never crashed as hard as cross-sectional stock momentum. Trend did well in most big equity drawdowns, especially protracted ones. Trend flat in the 2010s because trends were short (central banks curtailed trends); commodity losses offset bond gains.
- Extensions and warnings: macro momentum correlation about 0.4 with trend, similar performance (Brooks 2017); flow momentum is zero sum unless following a subset (short sellers, hedge funds, Drechsler 2016). Many overlays (vol-dependent speed, news, reversal filters) raise overfitting risk. Momentum and trend are not timeable by own valuation or recent performance.
- Carry (6.3): carry = return if capital-market conditions unchanged (yield, rolldown, dividend yield, roll return, option decay). Currency carry (G10, short rates, long AUD/NZD/CAD, short JPY/CHF) since 1974. Carry Century composite SR about 0.8, double currency carry, due to diversification. Koijen 5-composite from 1983; broadest variant (Koijen 2018) SR above 1 before costs 1971-2012.
- Does carry pay? Cumulative carry vs cumulative return (Fig 6.10, 1983-2021): equity, bond, currency allocations align (whole carry earned on average); commodity carry clearly exceeds realized return (spot mean reverts after scarcity spikes); Treasuries, credit, options in between. Time-varying carry matters more than persistent carry.
- Risk by type: currency carry, credit, vol selling have negative skew and negative equity beta in crashes (worst: 1992, 2008); currency carry equity correlation rose from near 0 (1970-99) to above 0.5 (2000-20), credit spread carry positive, dividend-yield stock selection carry negative, equity country, bond country and commodity carry near zero. Carry timing (cut carry when risk aversion rises) worked in late 2008 but produced false alarms in the 2010s. Another idea: scale carry by cross-sectional dispersion of rates (narrow dispersion, smaller profit).
- Defensive/quality (6.4): flat security market line in US stocks 1931-2020 (arithmetic mean flat; geometric mean inverted: low-beta beat high-beta). Theories: leverage constraints (Black; Frazzini-Pedersen) and lottery preferences (Barberis-Huang).
- BAB: rank by beta (1-year daily vol, 5-year three-day correlations, shrunk), long levered low beta, short delevered high beta, ex-ante beta zero. CAPM alpha 9%, SR 0.73. QMJ: 16 metrics (profitability, growth, safety), dollar neutral, beta negative, alpha 6%, SR 0.54, IR 0.91 (BAB 0.80). Both keep 3-4% marginal alpha after other factors. BAB SR 0.38 large caps vs 0.85 small caps; QMJ 0.39 vs 0.63. BAB positive in all 49 US industries 1926-2012 and in all countries 1990-2020 (global SR 0.98, QMJ 0.70). Out-of-sample 2010-2021 still good (Fig 6.13).
- Dollar-neutral defensive (SMR, IVOL, QMJ) has beta -0.3 to -0.8, so gains in drawdowns, raw return near zero; market-neutral BAB is a coin flip in drawdowns (beta compression). Long-only low-beta (0.5-0.8) loses in market falls but beats beta-1. Value spread in defensive stocks has little timing value. Bond version: short maturities have higher SR than long (BAB in Treasuries and credit SR 0.8-1.0). Long-shot bias in sports betting is an example of the low-risk effect.

### Ch 7 - Alpha and Its Cousins (pp. 139-149)
- Active return vs alpha: no unique measure; CAPM alpha penalises beta, multi-factor alphas depend on chosen factors and rarely subtract costs. Randomness often overwhelms skill, even over long samples.
- Passive share: about 20-50% depending on definition, only 18% of global equity by broad view (BlackRock). Morningstar: US equity mutual fund passive share 19% to 50% (2007-2021). Passive can free-ride on active price discovery; effects on correlation and concentration.
- Systematic vs discretionary: average pairwise correlation of active returns 0.15 within each group, 0.05 across; systematic share of active equity under 10%.
- Active performance: Sharpe's arithmetic (zero-sum before fees); mutual funds negative net alpha; institutions, hedge funds, PE slightly positive but decaying and biased by survivorship. Hedge fund alpha decayed (Fig 7.1); beta about 0.3, correlation 0.8. Barth et al. 2021 (regulatory data): non-reporting funds do well, so databases may not overstate returns.
- Persistence of manager alpha is weak and modest; PE persistence gone after the late 1990s, venture capital persists. Dusty corners (EM, small caps, private assets) have higher alpha dispersion (PE/VC interquartile about +/-10% IRR vs long/short equity +/-3%, equity mutual funds +/-2%, bond funds +/-1%) and negative alpha is possible. Look for markets full of unsophisticated or non-economic players ("play with patsies, not sharks"). Active stock pickers do better in recessions and high-dispersion periods. Active fixed income managers all load credit/equity risk (correlations 0.6-0.7), which explains outperformance in the bullish 2010s.
- Demystifying: hedge fund industry 1994-2020 excess return 4.3% a year: 2.0% market premia (equity beta, correlation 0.77), 1.6% alternative risk premia (mostly momentum, trend, put selling, tech, small caps), 0.7% regression alpha; quality loading negative (-0.3%). Buffett: mostly market plus quality and low beta rather than value, plus some alpha. Soros (Quantum): fully explained by a handful of factors, mainly trend chasing. Caveat: factor returns are gross of costs and may be overfitted.
- ARP vs alpha: alpha morphs into known ARP over time (value, momentum, carry did long ago; fundamental momentum, quality may next). Key trade-off: overfitting risk in proprietary signals vs overcrowding and obsolescence in known ARP.

### Topic tags
- trend: 12m lookback with 1m lag SR 0.89 (1926-2020, composite); no lag 1.27; three-window blend 1.67; 1-month lookback worst after lag or costs; flat 2010s; smile payoff; positive in bear markets; works in illiquid asset classes too (IL ch.6.2).
- breakout: not covered.
- mean-reversion: value and contrarian (multi-year), short-term reversal arbitraged away by mid-2000s (SR 0.77 on paper), long-term reversal SR 0.27; rebalancing as contrarian; contrarian market timing flashes early (IL 6.1).
- momentum/oscillators: stock momentum 12-1 months; crashes after market turns (1932, 2009, Nov 2020); macro and earnings momentum; factor momentum; momentum mostly earned overnight (Lou-Polk-Skouros, Box 5.2).
- patterns: none (author is skeptical of tinkering overlays).
- intraday/sessions: overnight vs intraday split: equity premium and all momentum profit earned overnight, defensive strategies better in day; overnight predicts next overnight positively, next intraday negatively; retail morning vs institution close tug of war (Box 5.2).
- volatility: vol selling is the ultimate carry with compensation for risk; vol-targeting across assets and time is key to trend SRs; Barroso-Santa-Clara vol-managed momentum reduces crashes.
- seasonality/time: same-month seasonality (stronger in factors), announcement-day premium (largely pre-announcement), turn-of-month (dash for cash, Etula 2020), FOMC-day equity strength, January and Halloween (2011 book); author says costs make most of these unimplementable (Box 5.2).
- stops-and-exits: not covered; trend positions are vol-sized, exit by signal sign.
- position-sizing/risk: vol targeting, market-neutral vs dollar-neutral construction changes drawdown behavior; beta hedging reduces momentum crashes; cut carry when dispersion narrow.
- testing-and-robustness/overfitting: criteria persistent, pervasive, robust; use deliberately simple variants; century and out-of-sample (1866-1926, 1826-71) evidence; overlays raise overfitting; proprietary signals overfit, ARP obsolete; factor regressions are sample specific; PE verdicts seesaw with benchmark choice; IRR gameable; factor returns gross of costs.
- costs/execution: all style results are gross; high-turnover strategies (momentum, BAB, Pastor-Stambaugh, fast trend) lose the most; PE all-in fees 5-6%; small-cap factor edge offset by cost and capacity; liquidity-provision strategies capacity constrained.
- psychology/discipline: smoothness preference, overconfidence in manager picking, extrapolating windfall returns, value capitulation ("investors lose faith at the bottom"), reach for yield.
- portfolio/diversification: value and momentum correlation -0.5; styles across asset classes diversify; multi-asset carry SR double single currency carry; composite/multi-metric signals more consistent; manager correlations differ by sector.

### Warnings / pitfalls
- All style numbers are gross of trading cost; momentum, BAB, Pastor-Stambaugh, fast trend and short-term reversal are the most cost-sensitive. A lag and a simple design are used as a rough cost adjustment.
- Overlay tinkering (speed per asset, news, vol regime, reversal filters) raises overfitting risk, only add when paper gain is compelling.
- Proprietary alpha: overfit; known factors: crowded and obsolete. Every anomaly was most profitable before publication (currency carry, STREV, size).
- Sample-specific luck: a decade (2010s trend, 2018-20 value) can wipe out years, and true sizes are small relative to noise; PE and housing verdicts flipped with data corrections and benchmark choice.
- Value and contrarian signals are short structural change; momentum crashes after sharp market turns; carry has negative skew and rising equity correlation (commoditisation); dollar-neutral vs market-neutral construction changes drawdown behavior; momentum and trend cannot be timed by their own valuation.
- Smoothed or IRR returns understate risk (autocorrelation, stale pricing). Any regression on smoothed data (hedge funds, private assets) is misleading; use holdings or de-smoothed betas.
- Factor regressions: attribution depends on chosen factors; compare manager net returns with gross factor returns only with caution.
- Extrapolating windfall returns (falling yields, cap rate compression) into expectations is dangerous. Income yield is the ceiling on expected real return in real estate.
- Zero-sum flows: "flow momentum" ignores that every buyer has a seller; follow only identifiable informed subsets (short sellers, hedge funds), who are fallible.

# Part 3: ch.8-10: risk premia theory, four equations, predictive techniques


### Overview
- This part is the conceptual bridge of Part II. It is NOT a new numbers chapter: ch.8 asks why premia exist (risk vs. behavior, who is on the other side, crowding), ch.9 is about patience, "has the world changed?" and data-mining discounts, ch.10 gives four equations and a short tour of forecasting techniques.
- Stance: most premia are a mix of risk and behavior; Ilmanen leans behavioral for style premia because they work in many asset classes (risk stories are usually asset-class specific). Limits of arbitrage keep behavioral anomalies alive.
- Few new return-source sizes appear here (those are in ch.4-6, outside my range). The useful new items are: evidence on decay after publication, the "halve the gross Sharpe" rule, a Bayesian shrinkage pointer, the crowding-equals-valuation argument, the t-stat/years arithmetic, and the fundamental law with breadth.

### Chapter notes
### Ch 8 - Theories Explaining Long-run Return Sources (pp. 151-162)
- 8.1 Risk vs. mispricing: both camps have many theories and data cannot separate them. Equity premium and short-vol / catastrophe-insurance strategies are clearly risk-based. Trend and quality look behavioral because they pay in bad times (safe-haven payoffs).
- Limits of arbitrage: noise-trader risk (rich can get richer), trading costs, short-sale limits, and above all leverage constraints. Low-vol and highly diversified strategies need big leverage (5-10x for a diversified ARP portfolio to hit 10% vol), which few want.
- "No pain, no premium": the fact that many investors quit any factor after a few bad years is what keeps it from being arbitraged away.
- 8.2 Bad returns in bad times: a premium should be larger if the asset loses in recessions/drawdowns. Bad times = equity drawdowns plus contraction; common features are high vol, poor liquidity, forced deleveraging.
- Test on 20 premia (liquid asset classes, illiquid assets, long/short value, momentum, carry, defensive, trend, put selling; 1990-2020): only a mildly positive link of return to vol or equity beta (Fig 8.2). Sharpe vs equity correlation was about -0.3, so you did not have to give up Sharpe to get lower equity exposure. The sample (1990-2020) was disinflationary and friendly to risk assets.
- 8.3 Other candidates (Fig 8.3): Sharpe vs average return in the ten worst equity drawdowns - counterintuitive, some low-risk premia had higher Sharpe than equities; Sharpe vs skew - no relation (contrasts with earlier literature); Sharpe vs illiquidity proxy (autocorrelation with own and equity lagged return) - very mild positive. Sharpe vs volatility within US Treasuries, credit and equities (beta-sorted) - inverse, consistent with leverage aversion / lottery preference (correlation about -0.4 on the broader set).
- Behavioral toolkit: biased beliefs (extrapolation, overconfidence, anchoring, conservatism), non-standard preferences (loss aversion, lottery taste, leverage aversion), cognitive limits. Ilmanen's one-storyline for momentum/trend: underreaction to mundane public news first, then overreaction/extrapolation later, with eventual reversal (rubber band).
- Fig 8.4 (1990-2020): liquid assets and most premia show positive monthly/quarterly return autocorrelation (momentum) and mostly negative correlation with the lagged 2nd-5th year return (multi-year reversal). Real estate autocorrelation is huge only because of smoothing.
- Market ecology: extrapolators (trend followers, stop-loss sellers, vol targeters) vs. stabilizers (contrarians, central banks, rebalancers, target-date funds). Imbalance on either side sows the seeds of the majority approach underperforming.
- 8.4 Who is on the other side (Table 8.1 lists rationales and counterparties per style): value - over-extrapolators of growth / glamour buyers; momentum - inattentive or conservative investors; trend - contrarians; carry - tail-insurance buyers and skew lovers; defensive - leverage-constrained investors and lottery seekers; quality - tracking-error-minimizing managers.
- Asness et al. 2022 (unpublished, so only as reported): small non-13F investors are the other side of momentum; hedge funds are one other side of the defensive/low-risk factor; no broad investor group clearly tilts against value (the other side is inside groups, e.g. growth managers). Factor tilts of each investor group are directionally persistent, which supports sustainability. Retail being anti-momentum is confirmed in German and US studies (footnote 12).
- Adding-up constraint (Box 8.1): net flows of all investors sum to zero, so a change in aggregate preference shows up in valuations, not in net flows (only net issuance lets flows differ; QE and buybacks matter). So 13F or custodial flow data only tell something for subgroups, and the right question is whose flows are profitable. Smart money (hedge funds, short sellers) predicts returns somewhat; retail inflows tend to predict low returns. Same smart-money groups are the source of crowding/deleveraging risk. Gabaix-Koijen "inelastic markets": flows have larger price impact than the classic perfectly-elastic view.
- Crowding rule of thumb: check valuations. A crowded factor should show persistently richer factor valuations. In the 2010s there was no aggregate factor richening, only pockets (defensive stocks). Quote from di Bartolomeo (paraphrased): a factor does not get crowded, the type of investor holding it (leveraged, liquidity-needy) does.

### Ch 9 - Sustaining Conviction and Patience (pp. 163-171)
- 9.1 Impatience: investors expect consistency that competitive markets cannot give ("law of small numbers"). Myopic loss aversion partly explains the equity premium puzzle. Consequences: missing premia, turnover costs (also loss of high-water mark), and chasing recent performance at multi-year horizons where returns mean-revert.
- Fig 9.1 (AQR, normal returns, no autocorrelation): probability a strategy underperforms cash/benchmark. Sharpe 0.4: about 24% over 3 years, about 10% over 10 years. Sharpe 0.7: about 11% over 3 years, about 1% over 10 years. Fat tails and positive autocorrelation make underperformance more likely, mean reversion less.
- Significance arithmetic (fn 6): t = Sharpe x sqrt(years). Sharpe 0.4 needs about 25 years for t=2; Sharpe 0.7 needs about 8 years. (Our t >= 3.0 rule would need about 56 and 18 years at those Sharpes, which shows why live-track-record significance is unrealistic and why we rely on breadth and cross-sectional samples.)
- Berkshire example: beat the S&P by 9.5% a year over 44 years, yet 3-year relative return was negative 30% of the time, and by double digits annualized in three episodes.
- Patience is not always a virtue: momentum and the disposition effect mean cutting losers is often right; multi-year momentum can also hurt contrarians when structure changes. Ilmanen still says impatience is the far more common error.
- Ways to cultivate patience: realistic expectations, review broadly and rarely (avoid line-item thinking that kills the most diversifying or most capital-efficient piece), pre-commitment (written beliefs, action plans), conventionality, gradual entry. Smooth returns can be fake (smoothed pricing, peso problems, fraud).
- 9.2 "The world has changed": endogenous version (more arbitrage capital) should show as strong past performance and rising valuations. Ilmanen's counter-example: value did badly in 2018-20 while value spreads widened, so crowding cannot explain it. Exogenous candidates: Reg FD in 2000 (less gradual information leakage, maybe weaker stock momentum), central banks damping trends (hurting trend following), digital economy killing value. His reply: Fig 6.1 shows value working over two centuries of technological change; "this time is different" is usually overdone.
- 9.3 Data mining: rule of thumb for belief = persistent (long history, in and out of sample), pervasive (many countries and asset classes), robust (to design choices). Data-mining worry applies more to novel, proprietary, complex, nonlinear, ML strategies than to simple known ARP; the known ones carry crowding risk instead ("pick your poison"; he fears overfitting more).
- Decay evidence: McLean-Pontiff (2016) - US academic factors decay after publication but stay positive. Jacobs-Muller (2020) - no such decay outside the US. Ilmanen et al. 2021a - about one-third lower returns outside the original sample, and the decay was stronger BEFORE the in-sample period than after, which points to overfitting rather than learning/arbitrage. Jensen-Kelly-Pedersen (2021) argue the replication crisis in finance is overstated and offer a Bayesian way to shrink in-sample performance toward zero or a prior.
- Discounting: halving the gross Sharpe is a crude but acceptable start; subtract costs separately and discount more for distant data and complex strategies. Statistical multiple-testing corrections are useful but limited because the true number of trials by past researchers is unknowable ("cannot become virgins again with data").

### Ch 10 - Four Equations and Predictive Techniques (pp. 173-185)
- Eq 1: return = dividend yield + dividend growth + valuation change (identity; also real or excess returns; bond version: coupon plus duration x yield change). Carry-type strategies (vol selling, credit, EM currency carry, perhaps real estate) have a positive income part but expected capital losses that offset part of it. Falling discount rates produce windfall realized returns (past 40 years).
- Eq 2: CAPM, expected return = riskless + beta x market premium. Only systematic risk is paid; multi-factor extensions (Merton, Ross APT, Fama-French) were chosen for what was rewarded, not what needs hedging.
- Eq 3: mean-variance utility. Optimal weight rises with Sharpe and falls with equity correlation; at the optimum, marginal return per marginal risk is equal across assets. Constraints are often piled on until the optimizer gives the wanted answer; estimation error is the main practical problem.
- Eq 4: fundamental law, IR = IC x sqrt(N) x TC (Clarke-de Silva-Thorley transfer coefficient adds constraints and costs). Four independent sources with equal Sharpe double the portfolio Sharpe; ARP portfolio Sharpe could plausibly double by style diversification and double again by multi-asset. The sqrt math only holds for uncorrelated bets, and correlations rise in bad times. Slow market timing has almost no breadth, so it needs very high skill.
- Predictive techniques: constant (historical mean, debiased by regressing returns on contemporaneous valuation change - the intercept is what investors expected, Avdis-Wachter, Asness 2021) vs time-varying (starting yields, CAEY as expected real return proxy, DDM with valuation-change weight set to 0). Horizon: 5-10 years starting yield and value; 1 month to 1 year momentum, macro, news. Time series (directional, trend) vs cross-section (relative, market-neutral); contrarian market timing is most exposed to structural change because it needs a "neutral" valuation. Regression and sorting are near equivalent (Pedersen); Fama-MacBeth for factor rewards; characteristics predict better than covariances.
- Box 10.1: machine learning is evolutionary; limits are small data and low signal-to-noise; ML is more useful in risk management and trading-cost control than in return forecasts; needs theory to limit overfitting. Gu-Kelly-Xiu (2020) is the pro reference.

### Topic tags
- trend: behavioral, pays in bad times; hurt possibly by central-bank damping (unproven); extrapolators like trend followers are one side of the market ecology.
- momentum/oscillators: underreaction then overreaction; positive 1-3 month autocorrelation across 20 premia, negative 2-5 year; small retail investors are the other side of stock momentum; possible Reg FD decay (2000).
- mean-reversion: multi-year reversal in most premia (1990-2020); valuation mean reversion is the weakest, structure-sensitive timing assumption.
- seasonality/time: nothing in this range.
- volatility: low-vol assets had higher Sharpe within asset classes (leverage aversion); vol selling has income but offsetting capital losses.
- stops-and-exits: stop-loss sellers named as extrapolative flow; disposition effect.
- position-sizing/risk: MVO weights up with Sharpe, down with equity correlation; vol targeters are extrapolators; half-Kelly style items only mentioned in passing (growth-optimal footnote).
- testing-and-robustness/overfitting: persistent/pervasive/robust; halve gross Sharpe; one-third lower out-of-sample returns, decay stronger before sample than after; Bayesian shrinkage toward zero; multiple-testing corrections limited by unknowable trial counts; t = Sharpe x sqrt(years); underperformance probabilities by horizon.
- costs/execution: discount gross backtests for costs first, then separately for overfitting; costs vary by investor size; netting across strategies cuts them (ch.15, outside range); transfer coefficient.
- psychology/discipline: law of small numbers, myopic loss aversion, line-item thinking, commit in advance, review less often.
- portfolio/diversification: breadth math; style x asset-class diversification; correlation caveat; leverage need.

1. ★ **Crowding-by-valuation monitor for our own factors** - for each running stream (crypto taker-flow sort, Treasury month-end): track a slow valuation proxy of the factor (e.g. spread of the sorted legs on funding rate or carry, or realized net-of-cost return trend) and test whether a rich-and-flat state predicts weaker next-month returns. Source: ch.8 (8.4, Box 8.1), pp. 160-162. Claim: crowding shows as richer valuations; aggregate factor valuations did not richen in the 2010s. Fit: good for crypto perps (funding is a direct valuation of the crowded side), poor for the month-end bond trade (no valuation series, tiny sample). New versus failed families: not a price-pattern rule, it is a conditioning variable on existing streams. Risk: small samples; run a power check first and treat as report only.
2. ★ **Counterparty-type prediction (who must trade)** - sort stocks or crypto by the type of holder or flow rather than by price: e.g. weight sign of 13F-style holder changes is not available to us, but crypto taker-flow imbalance and COT positions (hedgers vs speculators) are. Rule: long when the non-commercial/small-trader group is on the "wrong" extreme and the persistent other side (commercials) is opposite; monthly rebalance. Source: ch.8 Table 8.1 and Asness et al. 2022 result (retail on the other side of momentum, hedge funds of low risk), pp. 159-161. Evidence: author reports only direction of tilts, no returns. Fit: medium; COT is free and weekly, tests on futures-like CFDs (gold, oil, indices) would hold with swap, so net result depends on holding period (weeks). Differs: uses positioning, not price pattern.
3. ★ **Defensive / low-volatility sort on US shares or crypto** - monthly: rank by trailing 1-year volatility (or beta); long low-vol decile, short high-vol decile (beta-neutral if possible); hold 1 month. Source: ch.8 Fig 8.3D, p. 157, plus Table 8.1 (leverage aversion, lottery preference). Claim: within equities, Treasuries and credit, Sharpe falls with volatility (correlation about -0.4 on broader set); hedge funds are one other side. Fit: good for the Alpaca daily panel (23,700 shares, cost-heavy shorts must be modeled; avoid micro caps); crypto perps have no strict equivalent but a vol-sort test is cheap. Differs: cross-sectional, uses daily data on real shares, not CFD price patterns. Caveat: the factor is well known and has had richening pockets (defensive stocks).
4. ★ **Debiasing historical means before reading a result** - for any candidate with a long backtest, regress its returns on contemporaneous valuation or discount-rate change and report the intercept; plus apply the "halve gross Sharpe" prior and a shrinkage toward zero (Jensen-Kelly-Pedersen) when sizing expectations. Source: ch.10 pp. 180-181 and ch.9 fn 14-15, p. 171. Claim: windfall valuation changes were meaningful even in 40-70 year samples. Fit: good as a method on our results; costs nothing. Differs: methodology, not a strategy. Feeds our "shrunk forecasts" rule; the one-third out-of-sample drop is a ready default for sanity checks.
5. ★ **Breadth planning for the portfolio** - use IR = IC x sqrt(N) x TC to estimate how many independent bets a stream needs to reach our t >= 3 gate, using the underperformance table (Fig 9.1) as a reality check on a live forward test. Source: ch.9 p. 166, ch.10 p. 179. Fit: good for power checks before data looks (Sharpe 0.4 needs about 25 years for t=2, so a single-asset test cannot pass; cross-sectional panels with many names can). Differs: design tool.
6. **Factor momentum / timing of long-short styles by own trailing returns** - mentioned only as pointer to ch.16 (outside range); idea: scale each stream by its 12-month return. Source: ch.10 p. 182. No author numbers here. Fit: medium, our streams are short.
7. **Trend vs. cross-sectional momentum comparison on crypto perps** - time-series (each coin sign of past return) vs cross-sectional (top minus bottom). Source: ch.10 p. 183 (definition only). No author numbers. Fit: poor-to-medium; trend on CFDs already died on costs, crypto perps have lower cost per trade but funding.
8. **Multi-year reversal as a slow contrarian signal on shares** - long past 3-5 year losers, short winners, monthly rebalance; weak negative autocorrelation seen across premia (Fig 8.4). Source: p. 158. Author gives no strategy return here. Fit: poor (daily shares data only from 2015, so 3-5 year formation window leaves very short test).

### Warnings / pitfalls
- Risk-based explanations do not fit trend and quality (they pay in bad times); do not assume a high-return factor must be risky, and do not assume any risk measure (skew, tail, illiquidity) explains Sharpe ratios - results were weak and sample-dependent (1990-2020 disinflation).
- Net flows must add to zero: a flow-based signal only has meaning if you can name whose flow it is and why that group is profitable or forced. Do not read a flow as "demand" without the other side.
- Crowding is best checked via valuations; leverage and liquidity needs of the holders matter more than the number of holders. Smart-money signals and crowding risk can be the same thing.
- Short histories mislead: Sharpe 0.4 underperforms in about 1 of 4 three-year windows; judging after 1-5 years causes ill-timed exits. But a decade-long drawdown can also be a real regime change, so the answer is pre-registered rules, not blind patience.
- Overfitting is worse for complex, nonlinear, ML strategies; simple known ones decay a bit but stay positive. Out-of-sample returns were about one-third lower; the decay appeared before the sample as well, which looks like overfit rather than arbitrage.
- Trial counts of earlier researchers are unknowable, so multiple-testing adjustments are only partial; discount by judgment (halve Sharpe as start).
- Fundamental law: sqrt(N) works only for uncorrelated bets; correlations rise in bad times; a higher Sharpe via diversification needs leverage to become return; transfer coefficient (costs, constraints) erodes the edge.
- Mean-reversion timing of valuation needs a stable "neutral" level, so it is most vulnerable to structural change.
- Smooth returns can be fake (smoothing, rare big losses, fraud); illiquid assets show lower vol and fake diversification.
- Footnote status: the key "who is on the other side" paper (Asness et al. 2022) is unpublished, so its findings are only the author's summary, not checkable.

### Chapter notes

### Ch 11 - Diversification, its power and dark sides (pp. 188-195)
- Fundamental law of active management (FLAM): skill x sqrt(breadth). Raising breadth is easier than raising skill. Equities: common risk caps the gain; long/short styles with near-zero correlation give much more.
- 60/40 is roughly 90/10 in risk terms. Equal risk in three uncorrelated asset classes with SR 0.3 each gives SR about 0.5 (factor sqrt(3) = 1.7). Risk parity needs 2-2.5x leverage to match 60/40's 10% vol (p. 189 footnote 4).
- Four styles in one asset class: per-style SR 0.2 -> 0.4; four asset classes: -> 0.8 (illustrative, p. 190). Vol vs number of assets figure (11.1): at correlation 0 four assets halve vol.
- Empirical (AQR, Ilmanen-Israel-Lee-Moskowitz-Thapar 2021, Fig 11.2/11.3, 1926-2020, gross of costs and fees): single style in one asset class average SR 0.4; multi-style per asset class 0.8; multi-style multi-asset composite 1.5 (nearly 4x). Rolling 10-year SR boost from style diversification 1.9-2.3x, asset diversification a bit wider, total 3-5x over time. Value-momentum correlation about -0.6; most other style pairs about zero; all-in style composite correlation to equities -0.1.
- Caveat stated by the author: 2018-20 was very disappointing even for the all-in composite (value stock selection the main culprit); paper numbers are before costs.
- List of craftsmanship features with a small edge (p. 192): long/short over long-only; many markets; several signals per factor; continuous signal-to-position mapping (rank-weighted, not extreme deciles); integrate factor signals before trading rather than mixing separate factor portfolios; sector-hedge stock selection; equal-volatility not equal-nominal weights; diversify rebalancing dates over time (avoid "rebalancing timing luck"); diversify execution over time.
- Box 11.1 rebalancing: all rebalancing methods beat buy-and-hold in a five-asset 4-decade test (Ilmanen-Maloney 2016); slower (annual) lets momentum play out; "often but little" (quarterly, move one quarter of the way) approximates annual with less timing luck and lower costs. Rebalancing is defensively contrarian, short structural change.
- Critics: "diworsification" mostly overconfidence; correlation asymmetry (higher correlation in sharp falls) is real within equities but international diversification still works over multi-year drawdowns (Asness-Israelov-Liew 2011); Bessembinder: 58% of US stocks 1926-2018 had lifetime return below T-bills, top 4% explain net wealth gain, read as an argument for broad holding.
- Dark sides: leverage need (nine sub-portfolios at 25-30% vol would need gross up to 10x), conventionality/peer risk, lack of stories.

### Ch 12 - Portfolio construction (pp. 196-207)
- Top-down questions come before optimisation: risk aversion, missed considerations (ESG, liquidity, leverage, liabilities), allowed universe. Unconstrained optimisers love illiquid alternatives (smoothed returns) and liquid alternative risk premia (ARP).
- Macro-regime table (Fig 12.2, 1972-2020, quarterly 12-month periods split into growth/inflation up/down): asset-class SRs swing strongly by regime (equities best growth-up/inflation-down, bonds best growth-down); beta- and duration-neutral long/short styles have far milder variation; 60/40 inherits equity macro exposure; risk parity and the style composite are more stable. Style SRs there are "heavily discounted" for costs. Regime sensitivities are contemporaneous and not tradeable; style timing by macro regime is "twice right" risk (Asness 2015). Exceptions: currency carry behaves equity-like; defensive industry selection is bond-like.
- Worked MVO example (Table 12.1, AQR 2015): EQ 5% return / 15% vol (SR 0.33), FI 1% / 5% (0.2), illiquid alts 7% / 10% (0.7), ARP 7% / 10% (0.7). Capital weights at 6% vol 34/62, at 10% vol 66/34. Tangency portfolio 36/64. Unconstrained with illiquids + ARP: 9.8% return at 10% vol, 159% long alternatives funded by shorting public assets - author calls this a model error, not a recommendation.
- MVO pitfalls: model error (non-normality, preferences, taxes, wrong question) and estimation error (expected returns most fragile). Remedies: shrink inputs to priors (zero, group mean, Bayesian), constraints, robust optimisation. Methods that avoid return inputs: equal weight, equal vol, min variance, risk parity (Table 12.2 gives the assumptions under which each is optimal). 1/N is surprisingly robust with small samples (DeMiguel et al. 2009) but makes the in/out decision critical.
- Heuristics: breadth plus implementation efficiency plus skill; multi-factor harvesters diversify rewarded premia and hedge unrewarded risk.

### Ch 13 - Risk management (pp. 208-218)
- Survival over volatility. Big risks: equity direction (1930s, 1970s, 2008-9 are the three worst), "four horsemen" (inflation, deflation, confiscation, devastation). Fail-fast vs fail-slow risk.
- Techniques: diversification, hedging, insurance, then dynamic risk control (stop-loss, drawdown control, portfolio insurance - sell into weakness, may help in persistent trends such as 2008-9). Leverage rules: lever low-risk and liquid assets, use futures, never combine leverage and illiquidity.
- Put vs trend (Fig 13.1, Jan 1985-Mar 2020, AQR data): multi-asset trend following (1-, 3-, 12-month windows, four asset classes, 10% vol target, net of estimated transaction costs, gross of fees) geometric mean +8.7%, SR 0.84, CVaR(5%) -5.7%, max drawdown -35%, skew 0.1, equity correlation -0.08. Rolling 5% OTM one-month S&P put purchase scaled to 10% vol (gross of costs): -6.4% per year, SR -0.61, max drawdown -92%, skew 3.5, equity correlation -0.64. Put buying lost over 90% of allocated capital in the 2010s. Trend works in slow protracted bear markets, fails in sudden gap-downs; put the reverse.
- Table 13.1: performance in the 18 largest US equity drawdowns over a century: put, trend and quality-minus-junk the most consistent hedges; also Treasuries and gold; put by far the costliest in the long run. Author admits trend's tail-hedging record is "too good to be true" and expects a more modest future.
- Options are expensive on average because implied vol exceeds realised and skew is priced; whether put buying pays depends on whether realised returns were more non-normal than option prices assumed (not the case 1985-2020).
- Volatility is a good start: many risk measures scale with it, square-root-of-time works when serial correlation is small, fat tails distort levels more than rankings. Risk is more predictable than return: monthly autocorrelation of one-month vol (from daily data) 0.6-0.7 for many assets and factors. Page (2021): daily data and short windows forecast risk better than weekly/monthly and long windows, even for longer horizons. High-frequency data give better risk estimates (Bollerslev et al. 2018).
- Illiquid assets: smoothed quarterly returns understate risk; annual returns better.
- Kelly criterion: high growth but big drawdowns; half-Kelly is common.
- Box 13.1 volatility targeting: steadier risk; may also lift SR if expected return does not rise with vol; works best for the equity premium and for momentum/trend. Presented as icing, not the main reason.

### Ch 14 - ESG investing (pp. 219-224)
- Frameworks (screening, tilting, integration, ownership). Theory: constraints cannot help; a "sin premium" arises after repricing; ESG-SR frontier is hump-shaped (Pedersen-Fitzgibbons-Pomorski 2020).
- Evidence mixed: governance signals positive (overlap with quality/defensive), environmental and social unclear; excluding sin stocks historically cost return (tobacco, alcohol, gambling outperformed); activist investors did well. ESG rating providers correlate weakly. ESG data have short histories, backfill and selection bias. Little relevance for our data except as a caution on backfilled signals.

### Ch 15 - Costs and fees (pp. 225-233)
- Annual cost = cost per trade x turnover (200% turnover x 20 bp = 40 bp per year). Costs per dollar traded range from under 1 bp (index futures, majors, Treasuries) to several percent (private assets).
- Naive cost subtraction understates a strategy (p. 227): netting across factors, partial trading toward the target (Garleanu-Pedersen moving target), and using a fast high-turnover signal only to delay or time trades for a slow strategy (Israelov-Katz). Fast alpha decay (momentum) needs more trading but can still be net-positive; optimum is net-return maximum, between "too fast" and "too slow" (Fig 15.1).
- Frazzini-Israel-Moskowitz (2018): 1.7 trn dollars of live execution from one large manager, 21 equity markets, 1998-2016, implementation shortfall: mean 11 bp (10 bp impact, 1 bp opportunity cost); value-weighted 16 bp (15 bp); large caps 9 bp, small caps 19 bp; relative to VWAP only 4.5 bp (understates). Price impact is concave in trade size (share of daily volume), so earlier linear models overstate costs. Companion paper: size, value, momentum and short-term reversal survive realistic costs; value and momentum more scalable than size; short-term reversal the most cost-constrained.
- Fees: passive equity 0-30 bp, active 30-80 bp, hedge funds all-in 2-3%, PE about 5%; Asness law: high enough fees ruin any good product. Fair-fee logic: charge alpha fees only on active (non-beta) risk. Taxes box: not used in the book's numbers.

### Ch 16 - Tactical timing on medium-term expected returns (pp. 235-243)
- Based on "Market Timing: Sin a Little" (Asness-Ilmanen-Maloney 2017). Valuation signal: cyclically adjusted earnings yield (CAEY), out-of-sample version compared with the previous 60 years, score 0.5-1.5.
- Long-horizon fit looks good: correlation with next-decade return 0.41 in sample, 0.30 out of sample; with next-month return only 0.05. Quintile buckets look compelling (Fig 16.3).
- But the traded contrarian timing strategy (monthly, OOS signals, S&P 500, 1900-2021) did not beat buy-and-hold "in our lifetime"; all variants (longer horizon, trade only at extremes) had long-run SR 0.36-0.39 versus buy-and-hold 0.38.
- Reasons: contrarian signals act too early ("early equals wrong"; delaying 6-12 months or waiting for trend confirmation helps); structural valuation drift (richening 1950-2000; bottom-quintile CAEY much of the last 25 years, so the signal was bearish almost always since the late 1980s); signals too coarse; a 10-year horizon is really a chain of 120 monthly decisions.
- Statistics (Boudoukh-Israel-Richardson 2019, 2020): few independent long-horizon observations (ten 10-year returns per century); overlapping samples add little; Newey-West understates the problem; Stambaugh small-sample bias overstates betas of persistent predictors. Evidence of long-horizon predictability is weak; priors matter.
- Beyond equities (Fig 16.5): duration timing, credit-spread timing and style timing show the same problem. Equity and Treasury timing from value, momentum, curve slope and combinations: all SRs positive, best only 0.3-0.4. Combining contrarian and trend signals is sensible. Slope, credit growth, variance risk premium listed as other predictors. Welch-Goyal: OOS equity timing predictors are weak; Campbell-Thompson: sign-restricted coefficients help.
- Style timing (Fig 16.6, 1926-2020): average single style SR 0.4; multi-style 0.8; multi-style multi-asset 1.48-1.55; best OOS style-timed strategy added less than 0.1 to the static composite. None of the timing results robust across subperiods and asset classes.
- Breadth point: slow single-market contrarian timing has very low breadth; better to use several signals, faster ones, many markets.

### Ch 17 - Bad habits and good practices (pp. 244-249)
- Multiyear return chasing: flows chase 3-5 year performance where returns mean-revert, while 2-12 month momentum exists. Goyal-Wahal: fired managers later slightly outperform hired ones. Dollar-weighted vs time-weighted gaps (Dichev 1.3% NYSE/AMEX, 5.3% NASDAQ) are partly artefacts of trending inflows (Fried-Ma-Wang).
- Figure 17.2 (US stocks, 1931-2018, Ken French deciles): past 2-12 month winners beat losers; past 13-60 month winners lose to losers (weaker).
- Table 17.1 maps behavioural biases to opportunities: overextrapolation -> value/momentum; anchoring and conservatism -> underreaction -> momentum; overweighting rare events/lottery preference -> low-risk anomaly; overconfidence -> overtrading. Discipline and commitment devices favour systematic investing.

### Ch 18 - Concluding remarks (pp. 250-252)
- Low-return world; keep strategic portfolio, lower expectations, reduce waste. Principles: realistic beliefs, size risks, patient, diversify boldly, cost-effective, govern well. Stable confidence; process over outcome; luck dominates short samples.

### Warnings / pitfalls
- Long-horizon predictability charts (time series, scatter, quintile bars) look far better than tradeable results; in-sample percentile ranks leak the future; always use a rolling out-of-sample history (ch.16).
- Overlapping long-horizon observations give few independent data points; Newey-West understates the problem; persistent price-scaled predictors bias betas upward.
- Contrarian signals fire early; combine with trend or delay; structural drift (valuation regime change) makes full-sample "cheapness" misleading.
- Paper composite SRs (1.5) are gross of costs and fees; 2018-20 was bad even for diversified style composites; the macro-regime SRs shown were heavily discounted by the author.
- Expected returns are the most error-prone MVO input; unconstrained optimisation loads up on illiquid or levered diversifiers; shrink inputs.
- Cost estimates depend on the model: linear impact overstates cost for patient traders; VWAP benchmarks understate impact; costs spike in volatile periods.
- Put buying and short-vol both defeat investor patience; trend's tail-hedge record is partly hindsight and may be weaker going forward.
- Narrow, line-item thinking and stories bias judgement; process over outcome, since luck dominates short samples (ch.17-18).
