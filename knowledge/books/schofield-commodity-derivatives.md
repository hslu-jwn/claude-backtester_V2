# Part 1: ch.4 gold and ch.6 crude oil


Citation note: the extracted text has no page headers or footers, so book page numbers could not be read. Citations below use section numbers (SC s.4.3.1 etc.) and text-file line numbers (L) for lookup.

### Overview
- Ch.4 (L4258-6094) and Ch.6 (L8203-12087) are a market-structure and hedging-practice textbook written from a bank-desk view. They contain no backtests, no return statistics and no trading rules with evidence. Their value for us is the map of WHO must trade, WHEN, and WHY.
- Gold: no tight link to physical supply and demand, because almost all gold ever mined still exists above ground. Price is driven by currency-like and macro factors (real yields, USD, ETP holdings, momentum). Forward price is pure cost of carry (spot plus USD rate minus lease rate), not a forecast.
- Crude: price is refinery-driven demand. Seasonality (heating oil in winter, gasoline June-September), inventory and storage limits (Cushing), refinery outages (hurricanes) and speculative / index flows are the named drivers. Term structure splits into 0-18 months (physical: stocks, storage, supply security) and beyond (financial: rates, inflation, capex).
- Forced or scheduled flows named by the book: producer hedge selling and de-hedging (gold), central bank sales / purchases (gold), twice-daily LBMA auction, refiner crack-spread hedging (3:2:1), airline and consumer jet fuel hedging, monthly-average pricing formulas with daily hedge unwinds, futures expiry rules (WTI expires 3 business days before the 25th of the prior month), investor futures rolls (index swaps on the GSCI, ETFs), storage owners trading calendar spreads.
- Author stance: speculators have some price impact but are not dominant; hedgers and physical constraints matter more. The author warns repeatedly that hedges and relationships (regressions, crack spreads, differentials) are imperfect and drift.
- For us: mostly confirms mechanisms we already tested (GSCI roll, USO roll, crack carry). A few new angles: hedger positioning from COT, storage-utilisation state variable, seasonal calendar spreads, gold fix and gold-real-yield link.

### Chapter notes

### Ch 4 - Gold (SC s.4.1-4.7; L4258-6094)
- s.4.1 Market structure: London is the OTC hub (LBMA, about USD 70bn per day, small next to FX at USD 6.5trn; the author likens gold turnover to CAD or CHF). Physical standard: London Good Delivery bar, 350-430 oz, 995 fineness. Settlement T+2, loco London default. Unallocated vs allocated holdings (unsecured credit claim vs segregated metal).
- s.4.1.4 LBMA gold price: twice-daily auction (10:30 and 15:00 London) replaced the old fixing. Administrator proposes a price, participants submit buy / sell volumes within 30 seconds, price moves if imbalance exceeds 10,000 oz, repeats until balanced, then all volume trades at that single price. Used as a settlement benchmark by central banks, industrial contracts and options (Asian average-of-LBMA structures). So a forced, price-insensitive volume window exists at 10:30 and 15:00 London.
- s.4.2 Drivers: no direct link to physical balance (huge above-ground stock). Supply: mine output (China largest, about 11% of 2019 mine supply; South Africa fell from about 70% in the 1970s to about 3%), recycled scrap, central banks. Demand: jewellery (about 44% in 2019, price-sensitive, falls when price rises), investment (ETPs, peak 1,763 t in 2011), industrial, central banks.
- Central banks: net sellers about 4,400 t over 1990-2009 pushed price toward USD 250; Washington Agreement (1999, renewed, cap 400 t per year) coordinated sales; since the mid-2000s central banks are net buyers (emerging markets, China 600 t in 2009 to 1,948 t in 2020). Historic regime fact, not a tradable signal on our data.
- Producer hedging: forward sales by miners were a supply source until 1999 (bank sells spot, borrows gold to hedge, which adds spot supply); de-hedging after 1999 (and 2009 Barrick-style unwinds under shareholder pressure) forced banks to buy spot, lowering lease rates to historic lows and supporting price. Hedge book changes are a slow, large flow.
- s.4.2.4 Relationships: gold vs expected inflation (5y5y breakevens) is unclear year by year; gold vs real rates (5y real swap rate) shows a much clearer inverse scatter (author's chart, no statistics). Gold also trades like a currency (USDXAU). Barclays 2013 regression for spot gold: highly significant = price momentum (+), USD index (-), physically backed ETP holdings (+), Euro Stoxx 50 (-), MSCI EM (+); significant = US 10y breakevens (+), Michigan sentiment (-), US 10y real yields (-), presidential approval (-), industrial production of 30 countries (+). No R-squared or out-of-sample result is given.
- s.4.3 Leasing / forward formation: forward = spot x (1 + USD rate x t) minus lease fee. Worked example: spot 1,300, six months (182 days), LIBOR 2%, lease 0.25% gives forward 1,311.50. GOFO (gold forward offered rate) = LIBOR minus lease rate; GOFO is quoted as annualised spot-to-forward difference; positive GOFO is normal. Implied lease = LIBOR minus GOFO. Lease market: central banks and institutions lend; borrowers are producers' hedge counterparties, fabricators (cheaper than USD financing), and short sellers. Producer hedging drives the long end (12 months+), central bank lending the short end.
- s.4.4 Hedging products (forwards, spot-deferred, flat-rate forwards, barrier reset and knock-out swaps, puts, Asian puts, barriers, two-asset barriers): product mechanics, no price-impact claims. Useful fact: producer hedge unwind costs are collateral calls in rallies, which strained miners (2009).
- s.4.5 Trading gold: gold swaps are economically USD borrowing collateralised with gold. Implied lease rate can go negative (example: 2-month LIBOR 1.63%, GOFO 1.73% gives lease -0.10%; 12-month example swap 3.46% vs LIBOR 3% gives -0.46%) when demand for collateralised USD funding is high (a cross-currency-basis-like effect). NDF / bullet swaps and deferred margin accounts (leveraged, 8% margin, bank hedges by selling futures and keeps carry exposure) are explained.
- s.4.6 Yield enhancement: central banks sell covered calls on reserves (example: spot 1,300, 12-month 1,400 strike call, premium 18.35). Supply of call selling by official holders; no data for us.
- s.4.7 Summary: sentiment is unusually important in gold; strongest macro link is real yields.

### Ch 6 - Crude Oil (SC s.6.1-6.9; L8203-12087)
- s.6.2-6.4 Physical and refining: value of crude lies in the product slate. EIA 2017 yield per 42-gallon barrel: gasoline 20 gal, ULSD 11, other 6, jet 4, gas liquids 2, heavy fuel oil 1. Refinery gain: about 45 gallons of product per 42-gallon barrel. Gross product worth minus crude cost = margin (worked example Forties 2.40 vs Oseberg 1.50 per bbl). Crude differentials (light sweet vs heavy sour) should equal the product-slate value difference.
- s.6.4.7 Crack spreads: indicator margins per region (US Gulf Coast sour cracking, Singapore hydrocracking, NW Europe light sweet cracking). 3-2-1 crack = 3 crude to 2 gasoline plus 1 distillate (1:0.67:0.33). Example: crude 50, gasoline 1.40 per gal, heating oil 1.20 per gal, 42 gal per bbl. US margins tend to be higher than Europe / Asia because US capacity is constrained.
- s.6.4.8 Refinery profit drivers (external): product demand, inventories (inverse link to price), seasonality, import / export ability, fuel substitution, spec changes, weather.
- s.6.5 Supply / demand data (BP Statistical Review 2020): proved reserves 684 tmb (1980) to 1,734 tmb (2019); R/P about 50 years; production 95.2 mb/d in 2019; US shale lifted output from 6.8 mb/d (2008) to over 13 mb/d (2019); Brent field output fell from 2.9 mb/d (1999) to 1.1 mb/d; Asia-Pacific 37% of consumption; China about 14%; Middle East 23% of reserves, 32% of production, 10% of consumption.
- s.6.6 Price drivers: macro (GDP, strategic reserves, substitution, tax, EV adoption, USD), speculation and index investment, weather. Seasonal: heating oil in winter, US driving season roughly June-September lifts gasoline demand; crude grades that yield more heating oil gain in winter, more gasoline in summer. Hurricanes: Katrina (2005) closed about 10% of US refining and 90% of Gulf of Mexico output; Rita shut about 27% of refining shortly after.
- Index and passive flows (s.6.6.1): total-return swaps on the S&P GSCI make banks roll futures; the author says large index inflows can distort the shape of the short end of the curve. Passive long-only index money versus active "alpha" money.
- Storage and inventory (s.6.6.2): US storage capacity about 710 mb (2018). Concern about supply raises storage demand and pushes up forward prices; no storage space pushes producers to cut and lifts short-dated prices relative to long-dated (as written in the book; the sign statement is loosely worded).
- Davis (cited, s.6.6.4): curve segment 0-18 months is physical, beyond 18 months financial; term structure is more volatile than financial markets, may be seasonal; long-run mean reversion in crude prices.
- s.6.7 Pricing: Dated Brent assessed on cargoes loading 10 days to one month ahead; physical pricing windows are averages around the bill-of-lading date (example "2-1-2", five days). About 90-95% of crude sold is on term contracts, spot only 5-10% (anecdotal). Brent is a marker although only about 0.3-1% of world output; Saudi crude is not a marker because of 1980s net-back pricing history.
- s.6.8.2 Brent complex: Dated Brent, paper forwards, CFDs (Dated-to-front-line differential, quoted negative in contango), ICE Brent futures (expire last business day of the second month before delivery, EFP delivery or cash settle to the ICE Brent Index), EFPs.
- s.6.8.3 WTI mechanics: Cushing delivery. CME WTI futures expire 3 business days before the 25th calendar day of the month before delivery (pipeline nomination deadline is the 25th); physical WTI prices for 26 March-25 April refer to May delivery. Physical formulas use calendar-month average (CMA) of front-month futures plus a "NYMEX roll" term (average spread between delivery-month and front-month contracts over the 26th-to-25th window), so hedgers need futures trades across the month.
- s.6.8.3.2 Negative WTI (20 April 2020): May contract closed -37.63 (low -40.32, expired next day at 10.01). Cushing stocks about 60 mb of about 76 mb working capacity (about 79% full), with space largely pre-booked; open interest about 13,044 lots. Brent stayed positive. Author's story: longs that could not take delivery sold at any price, possibly worsened by large index / ETF positions needing to roll out of the expiring contract.
- s.6.9 Hedging along the chain: producers sell futures strips (basis risk between cash and futures; worked example effective price 68.28 vs unhedged 68.09); average-of-period pricing means the hedger unwinds a futures slice each day of the pricing window (1/3 per day for a three-day window); swaps on monthly averages; refiners hedge cost and margin: sell the crack (buy crude, sell gasoline and heating oil futures, usually product months later than crude months), or OTC margin swaps with weights (example NW Europe slate: naphtha 3%, gasoline 41%, gas oil 35%, fuel oil 21%; swap fixed at 7.24 per bbl). Airlines hedge jet fuel with gas oil futures plus a jet-diff basis swap, or with crude when no liquid hedge exists (correlation above 0.9 among Brent, gas oil, jet; regression beta e.g. 0.8; stack vs strip hedge). Storage owners treat storage as a put on the calendar spread; in contango, buy near / sell far if the spread exceeds storage cost; gas oil curve shows seasonal contango / backwardation (dearer in winter).

(Most evidence is "none given". All are candidate tests under our rules, with pre-registered gates and a power check first. Page numbers unavailable, section numbers given.)

1. **Hedger positioning (COT) as return predictor** ★ - Rule: weekly, use CFTC disaggregated COT (2006+) producer/merchant net position (or swap dealer net) for gold, WTI, heating oil, RBOB, natural gas, as a percentile versus a rolling window; go with the side opposite to the commercial hedging pressure (long when producers are least net short relative to history, short when most net short), hold 1-4 weeks. Trade dates: use Friday release time, never Tuesday report date. Source: SC s.4.2.2, s.4.4 (producer hedging and de-hedging), s.6.9.1 (hedging programmes). Author's evidence: none, only the narrative that producer hedging added supply and de-hedging pushed price and lease rates. Fit: good (free COT, daily gold CFD or EIA futures, few trades so costs matter little); gold CFD holds overnight so swap applies. Difference: positioning-based, not price pattern; closest cousin is our crypto taker-flow sort. Risk: only about 5 markets, 15 years, so small N; run the power check before any data look.
2. **Storage utilisation and curve extremes (Cushing)** ★ - Rule: monthly or weekly state variable = EIA Cushing stocks divided by working capacity (about 76 mb in 2020; capacity must be estimated and stated as an assumption). Compare front-to-second spread (C1-C2) to a cost-of-carry bound (financing rate times price plus assumed storage cost per month). When the spread is deep in contango beyond full carry and utilisation is high, test whether C1 earns subsequent excess return (storage-stress rebound) or whether holding C1 loses roll; when backwardated with low stocks, test long C1. Source: SC s.6.6.2 (storage), s.6.8.3.2 (Cushing at 79%), s.6.9.2 (storage as put on calendar spread: in contango store if spread exceeds storage cost). Evidence: single anecdote (April 2020). Fit: good for EIA contracts 1-4 daily (1983+), commission and tick model, no CFD swap; but storage cost per barrel is not in the book (only gas oil 7 USD per MT for 6 months), so it is a free parameter to fix before testing. Difference: state-variable curve test, not a calendar roll-date trade; risk of being the same carry family as I-121 (crack carry) - state clearly.
3. **Curve slope (roll yield) cross-section across energy futures** ★ - Rule: weekly, rank WTI, heating oil, RBOB gasoline (2005+), natural gas by C1/C2 or C1/C4 annualised slope (backwardation positive); long the most backwardated, short the most contango, hold 1 week or 1 month, unit risk by volatility. Source: SC s.6.6.4 (term structure), s.6.9.2 (calendar spread concept), forward price = carry (s.4.3.1 logic for gold). Evidence: none in book (the literature on carry premium is outside it). Fit: good on EIA contract data; only 4 assets, so the sort has very few degrees of freedom; test as a time-series sign rule too. Differs from GSCI-roll front-running (dates) because it is a persistent state signal; must not be confused with I-121 (crack spread level) - this is outright slope.
4. **Seasonal calendar spread (winter vs summer heating oil / gasoline)** ★ - Rule: in early autumn go long the winter (Dec-Feb) heating oil contract against a later-month contract, or long winter-over-summer HO spread; in spring go long summer gasoline over a following contract; use contracts 1-4 adjacent spreads that straddle the seasonal turn; exit before the near contract's expiry window (25th rule). Source: SC s.6.6.1 weather / driving season, s.6.9.2 (gas oil curve alternating contango and backwardation; example Jan 903 vs Jun 889). Evidence: book gives the pattern, not returns; whether the curve prices it away is the test. Fit: good; limited by available C1-C4 only (spreads cover 3 months ahead). Risk: calendar effects are widely known; multiple-testing ledger must count month x product combinations.

### Warnings / pitfalls
- Forward and futures prices are hedging-cost constructs, not forecasts (gold GOFO, lease). A contango does not mean the market expects higher prices (SC s.4.3.1). Roll-yield strategies therefore earn from risk transfer or storage frictions only, not from a prediction.
- Gold has a huge above-ground stock, so physical supply-demand balance is weakly linked to price; sentiment and real yields dominate. Regressions of gold on macro variables (Barclays list) are in-sample and give no out-of-sample test.
- Hedge ratios from regressions depend on sample, frequency, sampling time, maturity and conversion factors, and drift (SC s.6.9.3). The same applies to any spread ratio or beta we fit.
- Basis risk: cash vs futures, grade differences, delivery location (Cushing vs Gulf Coast vs NY Harbor), timing of average-price windows. Futures-only tests ignore delivery and basis effects.
- WTI is landlocked (Cushing); front-month prices can detach from global crude when storage is full (-37.63 on 20 April 2020). Any strategy long the front contract has a tail event; a test sample spanning 2020 can be dominated by one point (fat-tail / single-observation warning). Brent definition also changed (basket widening, WTI Midland added July 2022 at CIF Rotterdam), so Brent history is not homogeneous.
- Contract expiry rules differ across products (ICE Brent last business day of the second prior month; WTI 3 business days before the 25th; ICE gas oil about two days before the 14th). Roll logic in our data must match each market or backtests will mismatch.
- Many named flows are anecdotal in the book (index roll distortion, producer hedge supply); the author himself says speculation matters but is not dominant. Treat them as hypotheses.
- Costs: gold CFD swap is the financing carry (LIBOR minus lease); holding long gold overnight on FTMO pays that carry. Calendar spreads and cross-product spreads on futures cost two commissions and two ticks per round trip; the book gives no execution-cost estimates.
- Seasonality and storage patterns in oil are common knowledge, so they are likely arbitraged into the curve; test the returns of spreads, not the seasonal pattern of levels.
- Regime change: central bank gold flows flipped from net seller to net buyer after the mid-2000s; producer hedging flipped from adding supply to de-hedging after 1999; shale changed US oil supply after 2008. Any pre-2008 evidence may not carry over.

# Part 2: ch.7 natural gas and ch.14 commodity investing


Page numbers: the text file has no page headers, so pages are taken from the book's own index (approximate): Ch.7 about pp. 237-280, Ch.14 about pp. 462-497. Cited as SC p.<page> (index-derived) or by section number.

### Overview
- Both chapters are descriptive, practitioner-textbook material from a bank trainer. There are no backtests, no return statistics and no trading rules with parameters. The only numbers are contract specs, convention details and worked pricing examples.
- Ch.7 explains the natural gas physical chain (production, shippers, transmission, storage, supply, customers, non-physical participants), deregulation, supply/demand, pricing (gas-on-gas versus oil indexation, LNG contracts) and the derivatives (futures, forwards, swaps, options). The useful content for us is who must trade and when: shippers must balance daily, storage injects in summer and withdraws in winter, system operators buy and sell to balance, LDCs and utilities hedge weather and volume risk.
- Ch.14 explains who invests in commodities (index investors, ETPs, total return swaps, structured notes), how the S&P GSCI rolls (a fixed schedule, 20 percent per day on business days 5-9), what "roll yield" really is, and how banks hedge the products they sell. The author's stance: roll yield is not a gain made at the roll but the carry of the asset; conventional "roll gain" accounting is wrong.
- Overall stance: structural and mechanical. Flows exist because contracts force them (take-or-pay, balancing penalties, index rules, swap hedging), but the book never measures price impact of any of them.
- For our data (EIA NG/crude/heating oil/gasoline contracts 1-4, FTMO NATGAS and oil CFDs) the most usable parts are the storage-season logic, the contract expiry dates, the roll-yield decomposition (a measurement tool) and the observation that structured-product and ETF hedgers are forced, price-insensitive traders.

### Chapter notes

### Ch 7 - Natural Gas (pp. ~237-280)
- 7.3 Physical supply chain (SC p.~240-246). Shippers must balance injections and withdrawals every day or pay penalties; the system operator (TSO) steps in via nomination, confirmation and scheduling. Balancing tools: swing in production contracts, storage withdrawals at short notice, interruptible industrial contracts. Line packing (pipeline pressure) smooths intraday demand. Takeaway: balancing is a forced, daily, price-insensitive flow, but it lives in UK day-ahead markets (OCM, SMP), not in the US futures we have.
- 7.3.5 Storage. Depleted fields are cheap base-load storage (slow withdrawal), salt caverns are peak storage (fast). "Excess gas is injected during warmer periods and withdrawn in colder times". If storage is scarce relative to demand, a cold spell makes prices rise steeply; the book gives no number.
- 7.3.8 Non-physical participants: banks lending to producers, utilities with uncertain consumption (volume plus price risk, so they need flexible structures), industrials, hedge funds. The author lists them but gives no flow sizes.
- 7.4 Deregulation (US: 1938 Act, 1954 Phillips, 1978 NGPA, FERC Orders 436 and 636, 1989 Decontrol; UK Gas Acts; EU directives). Background only; the practical point is that US gas prices are market-driven since about 1989-1992 and Europe moved from oil-indexed to hub pricing.
- 7.5 Demand and supply (pp.~246-252). Residential heating drives the seasonal price pattern; electricity sector is the largest user. Global production 3,989 Bcm in 2019, US the largest producer (1,128) and consumer (846). US swung from net importer (109 Bcm) at the first edition to net exporter (123 Bcm) in 2019 because of shale (horizontal drilling plus fracking). R/P ratio about 50 years. LNG occupies 1/600 of gaseous volume; US, Trinidad, Nigeria export small LNG volumes to many countries.
- 7.6.2 Oil indexation (SC p.~253-255). About 75 percent of European contracts (EC 2006 report) were oil-indexed; about 80 percent in Netherlands/Norway/Russia, about 20 percent in UK. Typical formula "3-1-3": average oil product price over three months, lagged one month, applied for three months. So contract gas prices follow oil with a lag and heavy damping. LNG Asian contracts: price = A x JCC + B, A about 0.11-0.15, full oil parity is 0.1724 (so 0.1485 is 85 percent linkage); "S-curve" formulas flatten the slope at high and low oil prices. The shift is toward hub indices (Henry Hub, NBP, TTF, JKM).
- 7.7 Price drivers (SC p.~257-258). Supply: production and reserves, outages (Gulf of Mexico hurricanes, e.g. Katrina 2005, Harvey 2017), maintenance closures when demand is low, infrastructure (interconnectors, LNG terminals), storage capacity, export share, supplier concentration. Demand: weather, competition, electricity generation (gas versus coal), exports, storage injections (summer) and relative fuel prices. Anecdote: after Langeled opened, a warm day with LNG arrival and a storage shortage produced a supply of 344 mcm against demand of 234 mcm and gas traded at minus 5 pence per therm. This shows the price can go very negative when a forced supplier meets full storage.
- 7.8 Trading motivations. Storage strategy: buy cheap in summer, sell in winter if revenue exceeds all costs. Shippers and TSO are forced into the market to balance; producers hedge with derivatives. Contract maturities: within day, day-ahead, month, quarter, season (summer April-September, winter October-March), annual (calendar or gas year October-September).
- 7.9.1 Futures specs (Table 7.1, SC p.~263). NYMEX Henry Hub: 10,000 MMBtu, tick 0.001 USD (10 USD), monthly contracts listed for the current year and next 12 years, last trading day three business days before the first calendar day of the delivery month, physical delivery at Henry Hub (Erath, Louisiana). ICE UK: 1,000 therms per day per lot, month/quarter/season/annual strips, last trade two business days before delivery start; delivery spread evenly over the period. Basis contracts quote locational differentials to Henry Hub.
- 7.9.1.2 Storage calendar spread (SC p.~265). Long the calendar spread (buy near month, sell far month) earns the spread minus storage cost. Example: 1-month 50p, 2-month 53p, storage 1p gives 2p profit. This is the mechanism that ties the NG curve slope to storage cost and explains why summer-to-winter spreads sit in contango up to the cost of carry. Table 7.2 maps each participant to forced hedging actions (producer sells, consumer buys, storage holder sells forward, spread holder long near/short far).
- EFP example: producer and consumer fix a physical price off the futures price and settle the futures out of the exchange; shows that part of the physical hedging flow never prints on the screen price.
- 7.9.2 OTC contracts and swaps (SC p.~268-278). Vanilla swap example: 30,000 therms/day, 70p fixed, floating = day-ahead average (66.57p), monthly settlement. Basis swaps (Houston Ship Channel versus Henry Hub minus 0.15 USD). Swing swaps, gas formula swap (price = 0.5057 + 0.0302 x Brent(6,0,3) over EURUSD), chooser swaps, double-down swap (notional doubles if the floating price is at or below the fixed rate), swaptions, corridor (buy 7.00 call, sell 7.50 call; example uses 6.74 futures, implied vol 50 percent, premium 0.35 versus 0.20), Bermudan options, spread options (NBP-HH, JKM-TTF as LNG arbitrage). Implied vol range: UK gas 40-90 percent, North American gas 40-120 percent. These are bank structuring details; for us only the vol range and the bank hedging motive matter.

### Ch 14 - Commodity Investing (pp. ~462-497)
- 14.1-14.3 Investor types and horizons: leveraged accounts (banks, hedge funds), private/retail via ETPs, real-money long-only (buy and hold, no borrowing), CTAs, sovereign funds. Market size (Barclays): AUM USD 15 bn in 2001, peak 418 bn in 2012, 311 bn in 2017; initially total return swaps and ETPs dominate; precious metals largest sector, then energy, agriculture, base metals.
- 14.4.1 Diversification claim. Correlation of commodities with equities was -0.28 (1970-2005) and turned +0.40 in 2006-2016 (bonds -0.10 to +0.28; T-bills -0.01 to +0.10). Morgan Stanley explanation: correlation is negative when the economy runs near capacity (commodities scarce, margins squeezed) and positive when there is slack (demand shocks lift both). IMF commodity index peaked just over 400 in July 2008 and lost half by January 2009. Regime-dependent diversification, not a constant.
- 14.4.3.1 Risk-premium strategies (Barclays 2011b): curve (long deferred, short near, a term-premium trade), value (short overvalued near contracts in steep backwardation, long deferred), momentum, arbitrage, volatility (sell implied versus realised). The author notes the risk in each (curve shifts, slow convergence, model risk, vol spikes).
- 14.5 Commodity indices (SC p.~468-477). S&P GSCI: launched 1991, 24 commodities (6 energy, 5 industrial metals, 8 agriculture, 3 livestock, 2 precious metals), over 60 percent energy, weights from five-year production value, reset annually. It holds front-month futures. Roll rule: the fifth to ninth business day of the month in which contracts expire, 20 percent of the position shifted per day over five days. Three published forms: spot, excess return (includes roll), total return (adds collateral interest on fully funded positions, reinvested; TR is not simply ER plus T-bill interest). "Enhanced beta" indices change roll dates (pre-roll, post-roll), use longer-dated contracts, or go long/short; "active alpha" adds momentum and vol.
- 14.5.4 "The myth of the roll yield" (SC p.~471-477). Key correction from Campbell and Company 2014 (also Till 2007, Frankfurter and Accomazzo 2010): rolling a futures position does not itself create a profit or loss, because both legs are marked at the same close. Roll yield = futures return minus spot return = the net carry (financing minus lease/storage benefit) of owning the asset. Gold example, 10 days, spot +45.00: contango (3 percent financing, 1 percent lease) futures +44.53 (roll yield -0.46); backwardation (1 percent financing, 3 percent lease) futures +45.47 (+0.47). Decomposition: roll yield = basis return + cumulative roll adjustment, where the roll adjustment is the near-minus-deferred price at the roll date (-1.70 in the example) and basis return is the change in (futures minus spot) (+1.23). Because the basis is bounded by the curve shape, over long horizons the cumulative roll adjustment dominates, so the old rule of thumb (contango hurts longs, backwardation helps) survives even though its usual explanation is wrong.
- 14.6 Total return swaps. Receive index return (e.g. GSCI TR) minus fee 0.25 percent per year, pay T-bill auction rate on a notional that is adjusted monthly with the index level; bank hedges by buying the index futures in index weights. The fee covers hedging cost. Unfunded versus funded (note) versions; counterparty credit risk on the bank.
- 14.7 ETPs. ETC (physically backed, e.g. Gold Bullion Securities, 0.40 percent fee taken as a daily shrinking gold entitlement; 99.55 percent entitlement in 2005, 93.6 percent by 2020 in the text), ETF (futures or swap based), ETN (unsecured bank debt, e.g. Barclays gold three-month index note: USD 25 par, matures 2049, issuer call and investor put). Authorised participants create and redeem and make markets. Synthetic 2x daily leveraged gold ETF: fully funded collateralised swap with notional twice the investment, fees 0.98 + 1.3 + 0.05 percent per year; value falls to zero if the index drops 50 percent. In May 2020 WisdomTree had to terminate several oil ETFs when a swap counterparty exercised its right to terminate after crude went negative.
- 14.8 Structured products (SC p.~486-497). Capital-protected note = zero-coupon bond plus call. 5-year, 100,000, 5 percent rate: bond costs 78,353, 21,647 buys the call; gold 1,500, 5-year ATM call 433 (28.87 percent of strike), implied vol 25 percent, so participation 75 percent. Participation rises with the interest rate and falls with implied vol. Commodity-specific structuring levers: curve shape (a call that is ATM to spot is deep ITM or OTM to the forward: in contango the 1,832 forward makes the call expensive; flipping the carry gives a 1,228 forward, premium 174, participation 187 percent), downward-sloping volatility term structure (higher participation), low cross-commodity correlation (cheap basket options; WTI-cocoa basket example). Other structures: income notes (bull spreads on Baltic Dry Index, max 5 percent coupon), reverse convertible (long bond, short down-and-in put; 8 percent coupon, 80 percent barrier, crude), autocallable (worst-of Brent and silver, 85 percent knock-in, early redemption at 100 percent, 3 percent coupon per asset above 100 percent), outperformance note (300 percent of oil minus gold return), worst-of call (coal and BDI).

### Testable ideas

1. **★ NG storage-season curve test (injection versus withdrawal), EIA NG contracts 1-4** - rule: each month, compute the slope of the curve (contract 2 or 4 versus contract 1, or winter-over-summer spread). Test whether the slope in the injection season (April to October) predicts the next-month return of contract 1, long front when the curve is steeply backwardated or flat and short when it is in extreme contango (carry/value sign). Exit at the next monthly roll, no stop. Hold cost: futures have no swap. - SC 7.3.5, 7.9.1.2, 14.4.3.1 (curve and value risk premia) - claimed evidence: none, only logic (storage cost bounds the spread, so extreme contango should be paid by the storage holder, not by a directional price move) - fit: good for EIA futures contracts 1-4 daily; uses data we have; sample is about 20+ years of one market so t-stats will be thin, so a power check first - difference: this is not roll front-running (I-115) and not a price pattern; it conditions on carry, i.e. who has to hold physical gas.
2. **★ Calendar-spread (long near, short far) versus storage cost, NG contract 1 minus contract 2/3** - rule: hold the spread long near/short far when contango exceeds a threshold relative to its own history (say top decile of spread to price), as in the textbook example (near 50, far 53, storage 1); exit at near expiry. Alternative sign: spread mean-reverts toward storage cost. - SC 7.9.1.2 - claimed evidence: arbitrage argument only - fit: good, spread positions need no outright view and cost is one spread per roll; but we can only trade spreads on futures, not on FTMO CFD - difference: a pure curve trade, not outright direction, not our failed outright families.
3. **★ Roll-yield decomposition as a diagnostic for I-119 / I-121** - rule: for every futures return series of ours (crude, heating oil, gasoline, NG), compute futures return minus spot return (or front-versus-second contract return) and split it into basis return and cumulative roll adjustment (SC eq. 14.6). Use it to check that the "carry" in our crack-spread carry (I-121) is a real return source and not an artefact of near-minus-next accounting at the roll. - SC 14.5.4 - claimed evidence: gold numerical example (-0.46 contango, +0.47 backwardation) - fit: very good, uses data we hold; no new trading - difference: it is a method note, not a strategy; it makes our carry tests more honest.
4. **Winter-withdrawal onset and the demand shock (heating oil and NG)** - rule: in the weeks when the first cold weather forces withdrawals from storage (the book gives no date; US data would be about November-December), go long NG contract 1 from the first EIA-confirmed withdrawal week to the end of January; test against the same window in other months. - SC 7.3.5, 7.5.6 - claimed evidence: none, only the statement that storage is injected in warm and withdrawn in cold periods and that scarce storage plus cold causes steep rises - fit: partial; needs the EIA weekly storage report (we have EIA energy contracts, not necessarily storage) and a weather proxy; swap on NATGAS CFD held overnight - difference: not a month-end or roll flow; counterparty (LDC, utilities) has to buy for winter regardless of price, but a seasonal pattern is probably already priced into the curve, so expect it to be falsified like most seasonality.
5. **Oil-indexed lag: crude/heating oil leads NG contract prices** - rule: each month use the three-month average of crude (lagged one month, per the 3-1-3 contract formula) as a predictor for next month's NG contract-1 return; long NG when the lagged-average oil move is up. - SC 7.6.2 - claimed evidence: contract mechanics only (about 75 percent of European gas contracts are oil-indexed) - fit: poor for Henry Hub, because US gas is gas-on-gas priced; the idea is relevant to European gas, which we do not have - difference: cross-commodity lead-lag, not price pattern.
6. **Index-investor flow calendar (GSCI roll days 5-9, enhanced pre/post-roll)** - rule: already tested as I-115 (front-running the GSCI roll) and falsified. The only addition from this chapter is that the roll is 20 percent per day on business days 5-9 for all contracts in the index, so the ER/TR distinction and funded versus unfunded forms do not change the schedule. Not a new test. - SC 14.5.1 - fit: spent.
7. **Leveraged/inverse ETF daily rebalancing flow (2x gold or oil ETF), end-of-day** - rule: the issuer (through the swap counterparty) must adjust exposure at the close in the direction of the day's move (leveraged ETFs buy after up days, sell after down days). Test whether the last hour or next-day return of oil or gold CFDs shows continuation proportional to the size of the daily move and to ETF AUM. - SC 14.7.2 - claimed evidence: none; the book only describes the 2x structure - fit: poor to uncertain; intraday sessions on CFDs died on costs, and the flow is small relative to the market for gold and WTI - difference: counterparty that must trade, but I-119 (USO roll) is already the closest test and was spent.
8. **Structured-product hedger flows (autocallables, barrier notes, capital-protected notes)** - rule: banks that sell reverse convertibles and autocallables are short the barrier put and must delta-hedge, selling underlying as it falls toward the barrier (85 percent or 80 percent knock-in levels) and buying as it rises; test whether sharp declines toward round barrier levels (80-85 percent of an issue-date price) in oil or gold are followed by faster declines. - SC 14.8.5, 14.8.6 - claimed evidence: none; the structures are described, not their hedging impact - fit: poor, because we have no issue dates, barrier levels or notional, and the structured volume is a small share of AUM (the book says structured notes are a small proportion) - difference: dealer gamma exposure, but untestable without proprietary data.
9. **Capital-protected note issuers are long volatility and sell it back (implied vol term structure)** - rule: the book states participation rates depend on the downward-sloping vol term structure and on the interest rate; test seasonal or rate-driven shifts. - SC 14.8.2 - fit: no, we have no commodity option data.

### Warnings / pitfalls
- Roll accounting is a common error: do not count near-minus-next price at the roll as a realised gain or loss; measure the futures return and subtract the spot (or front-contract) return. A study that uses the roll-date spread as "roll yield" overstates the effect (SC 14.5.4).
- The total return index is not excess return plus T-bill interest because collateral interest is reinvested and the collateral size changes with futures value; do not add them naively.
- Futures prices already contain financing and storage/lease costs; spot-versus-futures comparisons ignore them. A strategy that is "long backwardation" is paid carry, not a free roll gain.
- Commodity-equity and commodity-bond correlation is regime dependent (negative before 2006, positive 2006-2016); diversification claims from one period fail in another.
- Structured and leveraged products carry issuer and swap-counterparty risk; WisdomTree oil ETFs were terminated in May 2020 when oil went negative, so such flows can disappear abruptly, and negative prices break percentage-return models (our own NG/oil backtests need a rule for sign changes).
- UK gas traded at minus 5 pence per therm on a day of forced supply; physical gas is a market where price can go negative, so ratio-based returns need care.
- Swap and ETF fees are small (0.25-0.40 percent per year) but synthetic leveraged products add about 2.3 percent per year; any claim about an ETF flow edge must account for the fact that the flow is small relative to futures liquidity.
- The author gives no empirical evidence for any seasonal or flow-based trade; everything above is mechanism only and would need pre-registered tests on our own data (power check first, because NG seasonality has few independent years).
- Seasonality in NG is widely known; expect it to be priced into the curve (contango from summer to winter), so a simple long-in-winter rule will probably fail after carry.
