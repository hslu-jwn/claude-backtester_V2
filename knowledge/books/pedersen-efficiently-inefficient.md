# Part 1: introduction and Part I, ch.1-5: hedge funds, performance, backtesting, portfolio and risk, liquidity


### Overview
- Part covers the book's thesis (markets are "efficiently inefficient"), a map of the 8 hedge fund strategy families, the 5 investment styles (value, trend, liquidity provision, carry, low-risk/quality), and the Part I toolkit: what hedge funds are (ch1), performance measures (ch2), where trading profits come from plus how to backtest (ch3), portfolio construction and risk (ch4), transaction costs, leverage, margin and liquidity spirals (ch5).
- Stance: academic plus practitioner (AQR). Prices deviate from fundamentals because of demand pressure and institutional frictions; competition keeps the deviations just large enough to pay managers for costs and risk. Hence no free lunch, but repeatable edges exist where someone is paid for providing liquidity or bearing risk.
- Core test for any idea: "who is the sucker / who is on the other side and why will they keep doing it" (EI p.39). Two sources of repeatable profit: compensation for liquidity risk, and information advantages (fig 3.1, p.40).
- Part I has almost no concrete strategy rules (those are in Part II-IV, other readers). Its value for us is the method and the "forced trader" taxonomy: demand pressure from hedgers, index/roll mechanics, ratings-based forced sales, margin-driven forced selling.
- Very practical on backtests: lags, data-mining discount, out-of-sample, costs, portfolio-sort = regression equivalence, Sharpe/t-stat link.

### Chapter notes

### Introduction (pp. 1-16)
- Efficiency paradox (Grossman-Stiglitz): if prices were fully efficient nobody would pay to gather information; if naive investors dominated, beating the market would be easy. Truth: just inefficient enough to pay managers for costs and risk (p.4).
- Managers are paid for providing liquidity to those who need to trade: hedgers (farmers, airlines), investors raising or investing cash, mutual fund rebalancing flows, merger-target sellers avoiding event risk, pensions trading for regulation, banks preferring securities by capital charge, investors avoiding illiquid assets, and lottery-seekers (pp.4-5).
- Returns after frictions are near efficient, yet prices can differ much from fundamental value, because the price tomorrow can be even further away and liquidity costs are large (p.5).
- A discrepancy between price and theoretical value is either a trading opportunity or a wrong theory; you find out by running the strategy (live or backtest) (p.2).
- Strategy map: discretionary equity, short bias, quant equity (equity); global macro, managed futures (macro); fixed income arb, convertible arb, event-driven (arbitrage) (pp.8-14).
- Managed futures philosophy: trends start from underreaction, then herding gives delayed overreaction, then reversion; position flips when the trend switches (pp.12-13).
- Quant claim: small edge on many diversified trades; backtest "eliminates rules that never worked" but past success does not guarantee future (p.11).
- Five styles (Table III): value (risk premium plus overreaction), trend (initial underreaction, delayed overreaction), liquidity provision (liquidity risk premium), carry (observable measure of expected return; FX high-vs-low rates, bond yield, dividend yield, commodity roll yield), low-risk (leverage constraints: investors overpay for lottery assets), quality (slow adjustment) (pp.14-16).

### Ch 1 - Understanding Hedge Funds and Other Smart Money (pp. 19-26)
- Hedge fund benchmark is cash (absolute return), mutual fund benchmark is an index (p.21). Classic fees "2 and 20" with high water mark (pp.21-22); 12% gross becomes 8% net in the author's example.
- Database biases: backfill bias, survivorship bias, plus opposite bias because the best funds do not report; biases change average return estimates by several percentage points (p.23). Hedge fund returns are not market neutral, are negatively skewed with excess kurtosis (p.24).
- Roles of hedge funds: information, liquidity provision, diversification (p.26). Capacity of the whole industry is bounded because liquidity demand is limited (p.20).
- Rest of the chapter is legal structure (master-feeder, prime brokers, clearing): skip.

### Ch 2 - Evaluating Trading Strategies: Performance Measures (pp. 27-38)
- Alpha/beta regression R^e = a + b R^M,e + e. t-stat of alpha above 2 is "significant", below 2 could be luck (pp.27-29). Hedging with b gives market-neutral excess return = a + e.
- Sharpe ratio = E(R-Rf)/sigma; information ratio = alpha/sigma(e) or excess over benchmark / tracking error (pp.29-30). Criticism: reporting IR vs cash (no Rf subtraction) flatters (p.30).
- Alpha-to-margin ratio AM = alpha / margin = IR x (risk per unit of margin): measures return on maximally leveraged market-neutral strategy (p.31). Example: 3% alpha at 10% margin = 30% on capital, beats 7% alpha on unleverageable asset.
- Crash-aware ratios: RAROC (economic capital via VaR or stress), Sortino with downside deviation and MAR (pp.31-32).
- Arithmetic vs geometric averages; variance estimate with T-1; expected returns are very noisy even with long samples (pp.32-33).
- Annualizing: ER x n, sigma x sqrt(n), SR x sqrt(n). Table 2.1: annual SR 1 equals daily SR 0.06, four-year SR 2, loss probability 47.5% per day, 16% per year, 2.3% per four years (pp.33-35). Probability of a loss = Pr(N < -SR).
- High water mark and drawdown formulas; max drawdown as a risk measure; drawdowns trigger redemptions and tighter margin (pp.35-36).
- Stale prices: regress on contemporaneous plus lagged market returns, all-in beta = sum of betas (Dimson / Scholes-Williams); stale marking fakes low beta and positive alpha (pp.36-37). Relevant to illiquid series (our EIA contracts 3-4, OTC-like marks).
- Track record is net; backtests should first be judged gross (does signal have merit), then net of costs (p.38).

### Ch 3 - Finding and Backtesting Strategies (pp. 39-53)
- Profit sources (fig 3.1, p.40): liquidity risk (market liquidity, funding liquidity, providing liquidity to demand pressure) and information (production, access, news that travels slowly / behavioral limits of arbitrage).
- Limits of arbitrage: fundamental risk, noise-trader risk (cheap gets cheaper, redemption before convergence), riding bubbles because others delay (pp.41-42). So mispricing persists to an "efficiently inefficient" level.
- Market liquidity risk premium: illiquid assets cheap, higher gross return; liquidity-adjusted CAPM gives E(Ri) = Rf + E(TC_i) + four covariance premia (pp.43-44). Convertibles, fixed-income arb pairs earn this. Broad liquidity crises about twice per decade (p.44). Market makers earn the spread (pp.44-45).
- Funding liquidity: margin CAPM E(Ri) = Rf + beta*lambda + margin_i*psi (p.45). Investors who cannot lever overpay for risky assets, so safe assets leveraged beat risky assets (stocks, long vs short Treasuries, credit); basis for betting-against-beta and risk parity (p.45).
- Demand pressure examples (p.46, footnote 2): index additions, put-option hedging demand, commodity producer hedging, downgrade-forced selling by investors who cannot hold junk, passive commodity index roll (nearest futures rolled on the GSCI schedule: sell near, buy far), behavioral fads, merger target sellers (arbs sell event insurance).
- Backtest components: universe, signals, trading rule, time lags (do not trade at the close price that generated the signal; use a 1-2 day lag; macro releases and revisions arrive late) (p.47).
- Two rule types: portfolio rebalance rule (e.g. monthly top-decile B/M) and enter-exit rule (e.g. buy gold on a 20-day high breakout, exit on 10-day low) (pp.47-48).
- Data mining: every backtest is biased upward; discount more when more inputs and more tweaking; avoid current-constituents universe bias; parameters estimated on the same sample are an in-sample cheat; use split or rolling out-of-sample; prefer simple rules without specific parameter values; goal is robust process, not best backtest (pp.48-50).
- Cost adjustment: subtract expected cost on each implied trade from each period's return; matters more with turnover (p.50).
- Metatheorem (pp.51-53): a predictive regression equals a portfolio sort. Time-series regression = market timing; cross-sectional regression = security selection; coefficient = average profit of position x_t = k(F - mean F); regression t-stat ~ SR x sqrt(T). Fama-MacBeth: average the per-period cross-sectional coefficients, SR = mean/stdev, t = SR x sqrt(T). Multivariate regression tells whether a new signal adds value beyond one already traded.
- Warning: timing strategies are more prone to look-ahead (mean of F, tercile cut-offs use full sample); cross-sectional selection avoids it because ranking is contemporaneous (p.53).

### Ch 4 - Portfolio Construction and Risk Management (pp. 54-62)
- Principles: diversification, position limits (Chanos caps each position at 5% of NAV; students sizing 40% of capital in a "sure" arbitrage blew up on margin), bigger bets on high conviction, size in risk terms, correlations matter (long-short within industry), resize continually ("no memory, forget nothing") (pp.55-56).
- Mean-variance: x = (1/gamma) Sigma^-1 E(R^e); noisy inputs give extreme positions; fix with shrinkage, constraints, robust optimization; one-period optimizer ignores trading costs (pp.56-57).
- Risk measures: volatility (fine if symmetric, bad for short-option style crash risk), VaR (historical-sort method, but stale if positions changed; resimulate current positions over 3 years), expected shortfall, stress loss (pp.57-59). Two-sigma events are common and five-sigma events happen in real strategy returns (p.58).
- Strategic vs tactical risk target (5-25% annualized vol range) (p.60).
- Drawdown control (Grossman-Zhou style): require VaR_t <= MADD - DD_t (e.g. MADD 25%); cut risk as drawdown deepens, re-add when it recovers; riskier funds need looser limits to limit turnover costs; gives a rule so emotions do not decide (pp.60-62).
- Poker lesson: "strongest weak hand" suffers most; forced sellers define the bottom (p.61).

### Ch 5 - Trading and Financing a Strategy (pp. 63-84)
- Costs matter for profitability, choice of rule, which securities, and scale (p.64).
- Optimal trading by cost shape (fig 5.1, pp.64-67): increasing costs (market impact; liquid electronic markets): trade partway to the paper portfolio each period (example 30% toward target, 70% existing), many small orders; constant proportional costs (spread plus commission): no-trade band around the paper position, trade to the band edge, band width grows with spread; decreasing costs (OTC): trade rarely but straight to target.
- Cost measures: effective cost (execution vs pre-trade mid), realized cost (vs mid after trade, captures reversal), VWAP slippage (misleading if you are the whole market) (pp.67-69).
- Typical costs: 8.8 bp NYSE, 13.8 bp NASDAQ (Engle et al 2004 Morgan Stanley orders); small orders about 4 bp, orders above 1% of volume about 27 bp; another study 4.9 bp median, 9.5 bp value-weighted in US 2011, global 5.9 / 12.9 bp; about 40 bp at 10% of volume; avoid more than about 10% of daily volume (pp.70).
- Implementation shortfall IS = TC + opportunity cost, measured as paper portfolio minus live portfolio; trade fast when alpha decays quickly, slow in illiquid markets (pp.70-72).
- Capacity: net profit hump-shaped in capital; optimal size well left of the peak; style drift when diversifying (pp.72-73).
- Leverage definitions: long/NAV, gross, net (p.74). Funding mechanics: margin m with Pr(loss > m) about 1%; sum(m x price x size) <= equity; mark-to-market P&L; rebate rate below Fed funds, prime broker loan above it (financing spread); margin call; repo, prime brokerage, OTC margin, futures FCM margin (pp.77-80).
- Gambler's ruin = funding liquidity risk: the edge is lost if you are forced out; forced liquidations cluster when opportunities are best (pp.80-81).
- Liquidity spiral (fig 5.5-5.6, pp.81-83): shock, funding problems, position cuts, price move, losses, higher margins and tighter risk limits, redemptions, repeat; price drops sharply, bottoms when deleveraging ends, rebounds. Returns are non-normal; correlations jump. Fig 5.7: July-Aug 2007 quant equity (value and momentum) losses spilled to Japan and to currency carry unwinding mid-August.
- Predatory trading: others sell what a distressed trader holds, stop-loss clusters and short squeezes amplify moves (pp.83-84).

### Testable ideas

1. ★ **Partial-adjustment / no-trade-band execution layer** - Replace full rebalance with: new position = 0.3 x target + 0.7 x current (increasing costs) or hold while within a band whose width scales with the spread (constant costs); trade to band edge when outside. Parameters: 30% adjustment speed (author's illustration), band = multiple of round-trip spread (to sweep). Source: ch5 sec 5.1, pp.64-67. Claim: captures most alpha at much lower turnover (no numbers given; derived from Gârleanu-Pedersen 2013). Fit: GOOD, because our cost wall kills turnover-heavy rules; applies to the weekly crypto taker-flow sort (Strategy 20) and share portfolios and keeps swap lower on CFD holds. Differs: it is an execution overlay, not a signal; can be tested on any existing passed or unproven stream.

2. ★ **Demand-pressure map: downgrade and mandate-driven forced selling** - Rule: after an event that forces a class of holders to sell (e.g. rating cut to non-investment-grade; index deletion), buy the sold security after the pressure window and exit after reversal (author gives no windows; sweep 1-10 days entry lag, 20-60 day hold). Source: ch3 sec 3.2, p.46 and footnote 2 (Shleifer 1986, Wurgler-Zhuravskaya 2002 on index additions). Claimed evidence: referenced academic studies only. Fit: US shares daily bars (Alpaca) can test index add/delete only if constituent history exists (poor: no index membership history in our data list; would need S&P announcement dates). Differs: counterparty-must-trade family, not a price pattern; related to but separate from our month-end index flow test.

3. ★ **Liquidity-spiral snapback (forced-deleveraging reversal) on cross-sectional factors** - Rule: when a market-neutral factor (e.g. our crypto flow sort or a US-share value/momentum long-short built from daily bars) has an abnormal short-window drawdown across many names together with a volatility/correlation spike and rising margin proxies, increase exposure (or at least do not cut) for the following 5-20 days; weights shrink back as drawdown recovers. Source: ch5 sec 5.10 and Preface pp.xii-xiii, ch4 drawdown control pp.60-62. Claimed: Aug 2007 quant event, losses every 10 minutes then snapback on Friday, funds that stayed or re-entered near bottom recovered most (no numbers). Fit: moderate; testable on crypto perps (Binance liquidation cascades, funding, taker flow) where forced liquidations are directly observable via price/funding extremes. Differs: conditions on forced selling, not on trend or breakout; also an honest-sizing question (do not cut risk at the bottom) which our risk module can test.

4. ★ **Drawdown-control overlay (VaR <= MADD - DD)** - Rule: each period compute VaR of current positions (e.g. 95% 1-day from trailing 3 years or a vol model); scale risk so VaR <= MADD - current drawdown (MADD e.g. 25% of equity for a prop account, or the FTMO 10% max loss / 5% daily); scale back up after recovery. Source: ch4 sec 4.3, pp.60-62. Claim: gives a pre-planned reduction and re-entry plan (no performance number). Fit: GOOD for goal 1 (FTMO prop rules are exactly a MADD); testable on R-trade lists in risk/ by Monte Carlo of pass probability with and without the overlay, including re-entry lag and its cost. Differs: risk layer test, not an alpha test; must be checked against "no memory" argument and the risk of cutting at the bottom (idea 3 interacts).

5. **Alpha-to-margin ranking of strategies** - Rank candidate streams by alpha/margin (= IR x margin-per-unit-risk) instead of Sharpe, using real FTMO/futures margin per instrument. Source: ch2 sec 2.2, p.31. Claim: capital-efficient strategy choice. Fit: good for choosing among already-tested streams on prop accounts with leverage limits; no new signal. Differs: portfolio selection metric.

6. **Stale-price (lagged-beta) check for slow/illiquid series** - Regress each stream's return on contemporaneous and several lagged market/benchmark returns; report all-in beta and adjusted alpha. Source: ch2 sec 2.7, pp.36-37. Fit: useful for EIA contracts 3-4, Yahoo continuous futures with roll gaps, any series with delayed marks; honest-testing tool. Differs: diagnostic.

7. **Signal-adds-value double sort / bivariate regression** - Before adding a signal F to a stream already using G, run Fama-MacBeth cross-sectional regressions of next-period return on both (or double sort); require coefficient on F significant with t >= 3 given G. Source: ch3 sec 3.4, pp.51-53. Fit: GOOD on US shares and crypto cross-sections (many securities, weekly). Differs: methodology, prevents stacking redundant signals.

8. **Cross-sectional selection preferred over time-series timing (look-ahead guard)** - Use contemporaneous cross-sectional ranks; if timing is needed, use expanding-window percentiles of the signal, never full-sample means or terciles. Source: p.53. Fit: policy for tests; apply to our funding-rate, COT and VIX-basis style timing signals. Differs: protocol.

9. **Index-roll demand pressure (restated, spent)** - Passive commodity funds sell the near contract and buy the next around the GSCI roll schedule (p.46). Already tested and spent in our GSCI roll test; no new rule here.

10. **Call-it-liquidity-provision carry check on margin** - Margin CAPM predicts high-margin (high-vol) assets earn lower risk-adjusted return than leveraged low-margin ones (p.45). Test: within our US shares or crypto perps, sort by realized vol, hold leveraged low-vol minus high-vol (betting against beta), beta-neutral. Source: ch3 p.45 (cites Frazzini-Pedersen 2014; numbers not given here). Fit: moderate; daily US share bars and crypto perps feasible; shorting costs (borrow/funding) and costs of leverage must be modeled; weekly rebalance with partial adjustment (idea 1). Differs: low-risk style, none of our failed families. (Likely detailed in a later chapter; flag for the reader of Part II/III.)

### Warnings / pitfalls
- Backtests look better than live: crowding, regime change, and unavoidable data mining; discount backtests more the more they were tweaked (pp.48-49).
- Parameters estimated on the same sample used for performance give a cheating in-sample result; use split or rolling OOS; prefer rules without specific parameter values (pp.49-50).
- Unbiased universe needed (do not use today's index members); lag signals and respect release delays and revisions (pp.47-49).
- Timing regressions and tercile cutoffs are look-ahead unless computed expanding (p.53).
- High turnover strategies can be great gross and poor net; cost per trade rises sharply above about 10% of daily volume (pp.64, 70).
- Volatility understates crash risk for short-option-like or liquidity-provision strategies: small gains most days, rare large losses; five-sigma days happen (pp.58, 24-25).
- Annualized Sharpe hides horizon: a strategy with annual SR 1 loses on 47.5% of days; do not judge short-horizon results by feel (Table 2.1, p.34).
- Stale marks inflate alpha and fake a low beta (pp.36-37).
- Liquidity-provision edges are paid for bearing the risk of forced-selling spirals: positions sized without a margin/stress view get liquidated at the bottom (gambler's ruin, pp.80-83). Sizing 40% of capital in a "sure" convergence trade blew up in the author's class example (p.55).
- Funds that "panic last" (strongest weak hands) lose most; a pre-agreed drawdown plan beats improvised cuts, but cutting risk also locks in losses and can miss the rebound (pp.61-62; Preface xiii).
- Hedge fund database numbers carry backfill and survivorship bias of several percentage points (p.23): treat quoted historic returns from the book with caution.
- This part gives no concrete rule sets with return/Sharpe numbers; those are in Part II-IV.

# Part 2: Part II, ch.6-9: equity valuation, discretionary equity, short bias, quantitative equity


### Overview
Part II covers equity stock-selection: valuation theory (Ch 6), discretionary long-short equity (Ch 7, interview Lee Ainslie / Maverick), dedicated short bias (Ch 8, interview Jim Chanos / Kynikos), and quantitative equity (Ch 9, interview Cliff Asness / AQR). The author's stance: markets are "efficiently inefficient". Prices drift from fundamentals by an amount that pays arbitrageurs for fundamental risk, liquidity risk, funding risk and short-sale cost. Gaps are widest for illiquid, volatile, hard-to-short stocks and when active capital is scarce (EI p.89). The book is conceptual: it gives few numbers and no tradeable parameter sets. Concrete numbers are scattered (HML, BAB, Buffett, short-fee evidence). The material is mostly equity cross-section on monthly horizons, with the strongest mechanism content on short-sale frictions, leverage constraints (BAB), liquidity spirals (Aug 2007), twin-stock spreads, and market-maker economics. Little of it is directly testable at daily frequency on our CFD data. Most of it maps onto our US-share daily data (2015+) and crypto perps.

### Chapter notes

### Ch 6 - Introduction to Equity Valuation and Investing (pp. 87-94)
- Three equity styles: discretionary, dedicated short bias, quantitative. Quants: small edge on many diversified trades, model + portfolio optimization with cost awareness + automated execution (p. 88).
- Value investors make prices closer to fundamentals, but fundamental risk and liquidity risk keep a spread (the "margin of safety"). Prices wander furthest for illiquid, volatile, supply/demand-imbalanced and costly-to-short stocks, especially when arbitrage capital shrinks (p. 89).
- Intrinsic value: dividend discount model, Gordon growth V = (1+g)D/(k-g), two-stage DDM, residual income model (book value plus PV of earnings above k times book). Relative valuation (P/E vs peers), implied cost of capital, enterprise vs equity value (keep numerator/denominator apples-to-apples). Pure textbook; no tests.

### Ch 7 - Discretionary Equity Investing (pp. 95-114)
- 7.1 Value investing: buy cheap, short expensive; cheapness is often a reason (value trap: P/B can normalise via falling book value, not rising price; p. 99). Margin of safety: buy only when price is below the lower error band of the value estimate (pp. 98-99).
- 7.2 Quality: Gordon-style decomposition P/B = profitability x payout / (k - g); four quality groups: growth, profitability, safety, payout/management (p. 100). Good vs bad growth (asset growth/acquisition-driven growth is bad). Buy-cheap-on-value and buy-rising-on-growth can both work; sorts on P/B favor cheap, sorts on actual growth/profitability/safety favor quality; combining = quality at a reasonable price (QARP) (p. 104).
- 7.3 Buffett: Sharpe 0.76, 1976-2011, about twice the market's; excess return about 19%/yr vs market 6.1%; vol 25%; leverage about 1.6:1 from AAA debt, insurance float, deferred taxes. Alpha is mostly explained by loading on value plus quality (and low beta) plus leverage (pp. 104-105).
- 7.4 Holding periods, catalysts: value-and-catalyst investors, "creating a catalyst" (short reports). Disposition effect (selling winners too early) noted (pp. 105-106).
- 7.5 Activists: 13D filing at >5% stake; letters, board seats, proxy fights (pp. 106-107). Not tested in the book.
- 7.6 Trading flows and sentiment: trading ahead of large orders (front-running, often illegal) vs. fading price impact reversals after large orders (helps the liquidity demander); greater-fool/bubble trades; pump and dump (p. 107).
- Ainslie interview (pp. 108-114): horizon 2-3 years, 4 positions per analyst; criteria: management, business quality, then free cash flow to enterprise value. Net exposure about 45% (range 30-60%), balanced longs/shorts within industry and region to avoid macro bets; average long holding over a year; advantage claimed: ignores index weights, longer horizon than the quarter-focused crowd, stable capital. Exits when something better exists, not by price targets. Lost trade (NeoStar): right on trends, management destroyed it.

### Ch 8 - Dedicated Short Bias (pp. 115-132)
- 8.1 Mechanics: short seller borrows shares, posts cash collateral plus margin, earns the rebate rate; shorting uses capital. Loan fee: about 0.10-0.20%/yr for about 90% of US stocks; hard-to-borrow about 10% have fees from about 1% to several % up to 50% (p. 117). Risks: recall risk and buy-in, short squeeze, margin calls grow when price rises (position value and margin both rise; p. 118), funding liquidity risk. Bans on shorting in crises.
- 8.2 Short-sale constraints cause overvaluation: skeptics sit out, optimists set the price. Worked example (p. 120): two types of investors with same mean value 100; with no shorting and price set by the most optimistic, price becomes 110 (average of 80 in recession and 140 in boom). Loan fees get capitalised into price by lenders, so heavy short interest and high fees can coexist with overpricing (p. 121).
- Evidence (p. 121): high short interest means low subsequent returns; high loan fees predict low future returns even net of the fee; a rise in shorting demand goes with about -3% abnormal return the following month (Cohen, Diether, Malloy 2007). Short interest rises around SEC enforcement actions and earnings restatements.
- 8.3 Short-selling policy: benefits (price discovery, liquidity, hedging); the bank-run argument has little evidence (pp. 122-124).
- 8.4 Enron case (Chanos): red flags = return on capital about 7% pretax vs cost of capital about 9%, gain-on-sale accounting, opaque related-party transactions, heavy insider selling, executive departures (pp. 124-127).
- Chanos interview (pp. 127-132): check borrowability first; build the bull case first, then ask bulls to refute; key numbers: return on capital (operating income / net business assets), insider selling plus departures, unreadable disclosures. About 50 positions, each max 5%, average 2%, never leveraged; sizing by volatility, borrow and industry. Worst loss: AOL, shorted 1996 and covered 1998 after an eightfold rise, cost 5-7% of the fund, lesson: size by volatility and diversify across more names. Behavioral difficulty of shorting (constant positive news flow) is the main obstacle.

### Ch 9 - Quantitative Equity Investing (pp. 133-164)
- Table 9.1 (p. 134): fundamental quant (days-months, high capacity, backtest reliable), stat arb (hours-days, needs transaction-cost estimates and fill-rate assumptions), HFT (backtest unreliable: limit-order submission changes the market, "Heisenberg principle of finance", p. 135).
- 9.1 Value: any variable that normalises price works because expected return = cash flow / price; book-to-market is enough. HML (long top 30% B/M, short bottom 30%), 1926-2012: 4.6%/yr excess, vol 12.3%, Sharpe 0.4 (p. 137). Value works across countries and asset classes (commodities, currencies); value strategies positively correlated across markets (common risk factor?), negatively correlated with momentum (Fig 9.2, p. 138).
- Momentum: past 12 months skipping the most recent month, long winners, short losers; gross returns even higher than value, before costs (p. 138). Explanation: initial underreaction then delayed overreaction; also a quant version of the "catalyst". Value + momentum combination is better than either (negative correlation) (p. 139).
- Quality: positive excess returns in US/global, small/large; negatively correlated with simple value; combining quality + value + momentum is stronger (pp. 139-140).
- Betting against beta (BAB): the security market line is too flat (Fig 9.3, 1926-2010): low-beta deciles earn nearly the same average return as high-beta. Market-neutral construction: long about $1.4 of low-beta stocks, short about $0.7 of high-beta stocks, so both legs have beta 1. US BAB Sharpe 0.78; works in 20 countries, credit, bonds, equity indices, FX and commodities (Fig 9.4, pp. 141-142). Mechanism: leverage-constrained investors (mutual funds, individuals) overpay for high-beta assets instead of levering safe ones; Buffett and LBO funds do the opposite. Works within each industry (Fig 9.5).
- Portfolio construction (pp. 144-145): neutral by dollar and by beta, optionally by industry (industry-neutral raises Sharpe: removes industry risk, compares peers). Paper factors (top/bottom 10%, monthly) have too much turnover; real quants optimise signal-to-weight after costs. Remaining risk = the factor itself (value can lose).
- Quant event Aug 2007 (pp. 145-149): subprime losses and redemptions forced multi-strategy funds and prop desks to liquidate stocks, hurting crowded value/momentum longs and shorts. A simulated industry-neutral value+momentum portfolio, scaled to 6% vol, fell about 25% from Mon Aug 6 to Thu Aug 9, 2007 (more than 30 four-day standard deviations), then rebounded Fri/Mon. Signs of a liquidity spiral: smooth losses (down in 90% of ten-minute intervals on Aug 7), losses with improving fundamentals/analyst upgrades, value and momentum suddenly positively correlated, snapback. Response options: reduce, rotate, hold, add, flip. The stock market was up 1.5% that week; the event was invisible except through quant portfolios. Returns = mixture of fundamental shocks and liquidity shocks; risk models calibrated to fundamentals miss the second.
- 9.2 Stat arb (pp. 149-153): dual-listed twins (Unilever NV vs PLC, spread up to roughly -10% to +25% over 1980s-2013, Fig 9.7), share classes (BMW pref vs ordinary, discount to -60%, Fig 9.8), ADR vs local spreads (ADR spread is smaller because the arbitrage is simpler: fungible, same currency; Fig 9.9). Spreads persist because the trade ties up capital and carries currency/legal/corporate-event risk; spreads widen when arb capital is scarce. Pairs trading (Gatev et al. 2006) and reversal strategies: buy last days' losers, short winners; residual reversals relative to similar stocks (p. 153). Index/ETF/closed-end fund arbitrage: small spreads, big spreads only in closed-end funds, needs sophisticated infrastructure.
- 9.3 HFT as market making (pp. 153-157): market makers earn spread plus price-impact reversion for providing immediacy to non-synchronised natural buyers and sellers, bearing adverse selection and inventory risk. HFT arms race, stale-quote hitting, detection of regular order slicing (orders each minute exactly are exploitable). Flash crash 6 May 2010: a 75,000-contract E-mini sell ($4.1bn) executed in 20 minutes; the S&P fell 5.2% in 13 minutes; HFTs shifted to net selling at 14:41 and traded hot-potato volume among themselves; a CME stop-logic 5-second pause (14:45:28) stabilised it; liquidity vanished across ETFs and stocks.
- Asness interview (pp. 158-164): momentum found at 12-1 months; the most recent month shows reversal (basis of stat arb); validated by extending to 1926-1963 (untouched sample). Real-world differences: transaction costs bind, capacity limits, "time dilation" (a three-year drawdown of a Sharpe-0.7 strategy feels endless; pressure to change the model at the worst time). Core: value + momentum, extended to bonds, currencies, commodities. New factor tests: must make economic sense; survive out-of-sample tests across countries, time periods, post-discovery, other asset classes; test the economics (does the factor predict earnings, not just returns); survive costs. Three possible explanations of a past edge: chance, risk premium, behavioral/institutional inefficiency. Following the model with discipline matters: overriding it re-introduces the bias being exploited.

### Topic tags
- trend / momentum: 12-1 month stock momentum, long winners short losers; underreaction story; negative correlation with value; momentum works across asset classes (p. 138-139, 162-164).
- mean-reversion: one-month reversal in stocks; pairs trading and residual reversal as stat arb (pp. 152-153, 158). Reversal profits relate to liquidity provision (cited Nagel 2012). Value is a slow mean-reversion (5-year losers; footnote p. 136).
- breakout / patterns / intraday: not covered, except HFT (p. 153-157) and the minute-by-minute view of the Aug 2007 quant event (p. 146).
- seasonality/time: nothing.
- volatility: low-risk / BAB (leverage-constraint story); sizing shorts by volatility (Chanos). Return distribution is a mixture of fundamental and liquidity shocks; risk models miss liquidity tail (p. 147).
- stops-and-exits: Ainslie exits by opportunity cost, not price; Chanos trims losing shorts (AOL); no stop rules.
- position-sizing/risk: Chanos 50 positions, 5% cap, volatility/borrow/industry sizing, unlevered; Maverick net exposure 30-60%; quants dollar- and beta-neutral, industry-neutral (pp. 131, 114, 144).

- psychology/discipline: shorting is behaviourally hard; discipline vs overriding models; investors chase performance and flee drawdowns (p. 162).

### Testable ideas
(Our data: FTMO CFDs M1, daily bars of about 23,700 US shares 2015+, crypto perps with funding and taker flow, FRED yields, VIX futures, EIA futures, Yahoo 10y futures, COT. No borrow-fee data, no short interest, no fundamentals; check what Alpaca gives.)

1. ★ **Crypto perp betting-against-beta (BAB)** - Weekly (or daily), compute rolling beta of each Binance perp vs BTC (or an equal-weight crypto index; 90-day window, optionally shrunk toward 1). Rank; long low-beta perps scaled to beta 1, short high-beta perps scaled to beta 1; hold a week, rebalance. Leverage is cheap on perps, so the constraint story is different (retail leverage lovers overpay for high-beta alts, so the mechanism may actually be stronger). Source: Ch 9, pp. 140-144. Author's evidence: US stocks Sharpe 0.78 (1926-2010), positive in 20 countries and across asset classes. Fit: good; funding is in the data and directly measurable; costs are fees plus funding on both legs. Differs from failed families: cross-sectional, market-neutral, no price-pattern; relation to our Strategy 20 (taker-flow sort) needs a correlation check. Counterparty: leverage-constrained retail long buyers of high-beta alts.
2. ★ **US-share BAB / low-vol sort on daily bars** - Monthly: estimate beta vs SPY from daily returns over 252 days (shrunk), long about $1.4 of lowest-beta quintile, short about $0.7 of highest-beta quintile (beta-neutral), among liquid names only. Source: pp. 141-144 (industry-neutral variant p. 144). Evidence: Sharpe 0.78 US (long history). Fit: price-only data suffices; short leg costs (borrow) and size filter matter; the 2015-2025 sample may be short and affected by 2020. Caveat: well-known, may be arbitraged; useful as a "known factor" control. Differs: slow factor, not a CFD pattern.
3. **US-share 12-1 momentum and one-month reversal pair** - Monthly: long top decile of past 12-month return skipping the last month, short bottom decile; separate one-month reversal leg (long last month's losers, short winners, held a week or a month). Source: Ch 9 pp. 138-139, Asness interview pp. 158-159. Evidence: momentum stronger than value gross of costs; reversal is the basis of stat arb. Fit: data fits; costs in small caps kill reversal, restrict to liquid names; the momentum crash risk. Differs from our failed families: cross-sectional on shares rather than CFD time-series trend.
4. ★ **Residual (market-/sector-neutral) one-week reversal in US shares** - Daily/weekly: regress each stock's return on SPY (and sector proxy), rank last-week residuals, long worst decile, short best decile, hold 1-5 days; scale down when VIX is high (reversal is a liquidity-provision premium that is larger when liquidity is scarce, p. 153; Nagel 2012). Fit: good for daily bars; main risk is cost (turnover) and the quant-unwind regime (Aug 2007-type, where reversal suffers from unusual continuation, p. 146). Differs: market-neutral and residual; counterparty = liquidity demanders.
5. ★ **Quant-crowding / liquidity-spiral indicator and snapback trade** - Build a long-short factor portfolio (e.g. value+momentum proxies, or crypto sort portfolios from Strategy 20) and flag days when it loses more than N daily sigma on days when the broad index is calm, with no fundamental news (analyst upgrades not available, but use correlation switch: momentum-value correlation turning positive). Trade: after 1-3 days of unwinding, take the factor's other side or re-enter, expecting snapback (pp. 146-148); also use as a risk-off rule for our own factor streams. Evidence: author's single simulated event (25% loss in 4 days, recovery in 2). Fit: rare events, small sample, power check needed; best used as a risk overlay for Strategy 20 (crypto liquidation cascades have the same signature: smooth losses, fast snapback). Differs: event study of forced deleveraging, not a price pattern.
6. **Short-demand proxy for crypto perps: funding and open-interest as the loan fee** - Weekly: rank perps by funding rate (cost of the crowded side) and by funding change; long those with most negative funding (shorts pay), short those with most positive funding, net of funding paid. Analog of "high loan fee and rising shorting demand predict low returns (-3% next month)" (p. 121), flipped to the perp setting where the crowded side pays funding. Evidence: Cohen-Diether-Malloy -3% abnormal return in one month for rising short demand. Fit: good, funding data exist; check overlap with Strategy 20 (taker flow). Differs: carry/crowding sort rather than price pattern.
7. **Twin-share / share-class spread (limited)** - Where two listings of the same firm exist in our data (e.g. FTMO share CFDs and Alpaca shares of the same company; dual-class A/B shares such as GOOG/GOOGL, BRK.A/B in Alpaca daily), trade a mean-reverting log ratio, entry at a z-score on a 60-day window, exit at 0. Source: pp. 149-152 (Unilever, BMW pref discount). Evidence: spread varies 10-25% for Unilever; BMW pref discount to -60%. Fit: poor to moderate: US dual classes are tightly arbitraged, borrow cost of shorts, thin sample of pairs. Differs: pure relative-value on the same cash flows (no direction).
8. **Pairs trading / residual reversal on CFDs (FX, indices, metals)** - Closely related CFD pairs (US500/NAS100, XAUUSD/XAGUSD, EURUSD/GBPUSD), z-score of log spread over 60-250 days, enter at 2, exit at 0, daily bars. Source: p. 152. Evidence: none given in the book (cites Gatev et al.). Fit: poor to moderate: CFD swap on both legs and spread; overlaps with our failed intraday mean-reversion; mention as a control only.
9. **Short-risk sizing rule (Chanos)** - 50-name book, 5% cap, size by inverse volatility, trim losers (not add). Source: p. 131. Fit: only for any short-leg share strategy: use inverse-volatility weights and a position cap; no edge by itself.
10. **Value+momentum+quality composite on US shares** - Needs fundamentals (B/M, profitability); we only have prices unless Alpaca supplies fundamentals; could proxy value by 5-year past return (De Bondt-Thaler, p. 136 footnote: 5-year losers = value) and quality by low volatility. Fit: poor-to-moderate (proxy quality). Low priority.

### Warnings / pitfalls
- Value traps: cheap can mean cheap for a reason; P/B reversion can come from book value falling (p. 99).
- Crowded quant factors can lose 25% in 4 days with no fundamental cause; risk models calibrated to fundamental shocks understate this (more than 30 sigma). Value and momentum correlations flip positive in a liquidity spiral (pp. 147-148).
- Paper factor portfolios (top/bottom decile, monthly rebalance) have turnover far above what a real portfolio can afford; optimise after costs; small caps and high turnover are limited by transaction costs (pp. 145, 160).
- Stat arb and HFT backtests depend heavily on fill and cost assumptions; "Heisenberg principle": your own orders change the market (p. 135). Regular order slicing (same size at fixed times) gets exploited (p. 155).
- Shorting: borrow availability, recall, squeeze, rising margin on losing shorts, loan fees up to 50%; do not assume symmetry with longs (pp. 116-118, 130-131). Alpha in a hedged short leg may look like a loss alone (p. 118).
- BAB: dollar-neutral low-beta minus high-beta loses; you must lever the low-beta leg (1.4) and delever the high-beta leg (0.7) to be beta-neutral (p. 141).
- Spreads between twin shares persist and can widen before closing; corporate events treat share classes differently; capital can be tied up for long (pp. 150-151).
- Asness: a Sharpe-0.7 strategy has multi-year drawdowns; most failures come from abandoning the model at the worst time; only extend samples to untouched history to test data mining; test the economic mechanism and survive costs (pp. 160-164).
- Author numbers are mostly long-history US equity (1926-2012); 2015+ data may show weaker or different results (factor decay/crowding is not quantified here).

# Part 3: Part III, ch.10-12: asset allocation, global macro, managed futures


### Overview
Part III covers asset allocation and macro strategies. Ch 10 is an introduction: strategic vs tactical allocation, risk parity, market timing and its in-sample trap, and a return decomposition for each asset class (equity, bond, credit, currency). Ch 11 describes global macro: carry trades in every asset class, central bank monitoring, growth/inflation regimes, cross-country value and momentum, trade flows, themes, and Soros's reflexivity theory plus an interview. Ch 12 (based on Hurst, Ooi, Pedersen 2013) is a quantitative treatment of managed futures as time series momentum (TSMOM), with an interview of David Harding (Winton).
Stance: Pedersen is an academic-practitioner. He presents the edges as compensation for behavioural slowness, slow-moving capital and non-profit-seeking actors (central banks, rebalancers), not free lunches. Costs and fees are named as the thing that eats most of the gross edge. The interviews are qualitative: Soros gives no rules, Harding gives philosophy plus a few design rules (one model for all markets, trade slowly, research constantly). The Interview chapters add very little testable detail; the testable content is in the text of Ch 11-12.

### Chapter notes

### Ch 10 - Introduction to Asset Allocation (pp. 167-183)
- Strategic allocation: market-weight portfolio is the only truly passive one (no trading, everyone can hold it). Constant-mix (60/40) is NOT passive: it sells winners and buys losers, i.e. a built-in value/contrarian trade that needs a counterparty (p.169-170). Rebalancers are named later (Ch 12) as a force that trades against trends.
- Risk-based allocation: optimal notional is expected excess return / variance; risk should scale with the Sharpe ratio, so with a stable Sharpe the notional should be inversely proportional to volatility (p.171). Risk parity: weight classes inversely to risk (more than 80% of a classic portfolio's risk is equities); needs leverage to reach return. Argument (Asness, Frazzini, Pedersen 2012): leverage-averse investors overpay for high-beta assets, so safer assets have higher Sharpe (p.171-172).
- Market timing: predictive regression of monthly excess equity return on dividend yield, US 1926-2013: R(excess, annualised) = -5.3% + 3.3 x D/P, t = 2.8, standard error 1.2 (p.173). Conditional on D/P above median, next-month excess return is 11.2% (vol 21.6%) vs 4.2% (vol 15.3%) below median (Table 10.1, p.174). The author immediately says this is an in-sample cheat: predictor choice, "high or low" threshold and coefficient were unknown in real time; Welch and Goyal (2008) find most predictors fail out of sample (p.175). Persistent regressors bias the t-stat (Stambaugh).
- Equity return decomposition: dividend yield + dividend growth + valuation change (+ small cross term). Long-run expected return = yield + growth (e.g. 2% + 3.5% = 5.5%); US 1926-2013: yield 3.9%, growth 4.6%, valuation drift 2.4%, equity premium about 7.5% over cash, not sustainable (p.176-178). Earnings yield approximates the expected real return; Fed-model comparison with bond yields mixes real and nominal (p.179).
- Bonds: one-period return is about yield minus modified duration x yield change; yield-change forecast via roll-down (unchanged curve) is a decent average guess (p.180).
- Credit: return about spread minus duration x spread change minus default loss. Investment grade: spread about 1% vs expected default loss about 0.24%; speculative grade: about 3%/yr default, loss about 1.8% (p.180-181).
- Currency: return = carry + appreciation. Uncovered interest parity fails empirically. Long-run expected excess return = interest differential - inflation differential + cheapness vs PPP / horizon (eqs 10.14-10.16, p.183). Worked example: AUD +3% rate, +1% inflation, 5% overvalued, 5-year convergence gives about 1%/yr.

### Ch 11 - Global Macro Investing (pp. 184-207)
- 11.1 Carry trades (pp. 185-188): carry = what you earn if prices stay unchanged. Currency carry: long high-rate, short low-rate currencies; the "stairs up, elevator down" payoff, return distribution with a left hump (Fig 11.1, quarterly excess returns, Brunnermeier-Nagel-Pedersen 2008). Crashes happen in carry unwinds when funding tightens and risk aversion rises; all high-yield currencies fall together, so diversification does not cure it. Pegged currencies give "peso problem" fake arbitrage. Carry also defined for bonds (yield minus financing), yield curve (roll-down), commodities (futures curve slope, convenience yield plus index-investor price pressure; also calendar spreads in the same commodity), equities (dividend yield), credit (spread). Table 11.1 (Koijen-Moskowitz-Pedersen-Vrugt 2012, 1980s-2011, before costs): Sharpe currency 0.6, bond 0.8, commodity 0.5, equity 0.9, diversified carry 1.4 (p.188).
- 11.2 Central banks (pp. 189-191): Taylor rule R = 4% + 1.5 x (inflation - 2%) + 0.5 x output gap; with unemployment: R = 4% + 1.5 x (inflation - 2%) - (unemployment - 5%). Macro traders trade bonds/rate futures on expected rate moves, curve flattening on hikes, and currencies via the carry change. Because central banks do not trade for profit and smooth FX/rates, prices adjust slowly, creating trends (links to Ch 12).
- 11.3 Economic developments (pp. 191-196): four regimes by growth x inflation (overheated, Goldilocks, stagflation, lost decade) with asset-class implications; demand shocks move stocks and bonds opposite ways (rising demand: stocks up, bonds down), supply shocks produce Goldilocks or stagflation. Time scales: demand shocks within a year, capital/labour frictions 1-5 years, productivity and demography 5+ years. Qualitative only.
- 11.4 Country selection (pp. 196-200): macro momentum = buy markets that outperformed, short laggards; macro value = buy cheap vs expensive (equity index P/B, currency PPP or 5-year real appreciation reversal, bond real yield or yield minus own history, commodity 5-year reversal). Asness-Moskowitz-Pedersen (2013): value and momentum both work in equity indices, currencies, bonds and commodities, 1971-2009, value-momentum correlation about -0.35 to -0.46, combo is strong (Fig 11.4, p.198; no Sharpe numbers in the text). Terms of trade, trade flows and capital flows move currencies; political events are discretionary.
- 11.5 Themes (p.200): discretionary big-idea trades (China growth, sovereign risk, gold); no rules.
- 11.6 Soros reflexivity (pp. 200-204): two principles: prices distort fundamentals, and prices feed back into fundamentals (credit-collateral loops). Boom/bust has seven stages (inception, acceleration, tests, twilight, reversal, acceleration down, crisis); boom is slow, bust is short and steep because of forced liquidation. Rising uncertainty forces risk reduction ("liquidity preference"), and when it abates there is an automatic rebound.
- 11.7 Soros interview (pp. 204-207): no mechanical rules. Key claims: he buys into bubbles and only exits on a feel for far-from-equilibrium; admits timing is unknowable and that he usually underestimated how far bubbles run (lost money shorting the 2000 IT bubble too early). Sizing: very large positions only when risk-reward is asymmetric, e.g. a currency inside a fixed band (downside 2%, upside large if the peg breaks, 1992 sterling). Risk profits more than capital: raise risk after gains. Emerging markets: foreign inflows create boom/bust (flow-driven).

### Ch 12 - Managed Futures: Trend-Following Investing (pp. 208-229)
- Premise: managed futures (CTAs, about $320bn in 2012) are largely explained by time series momentum; gross Sharpe of diversified TSMOM is 1.8 (p.209).
- 12.1 Life cycle of a trend (pp. 210-212): mechanisms for the start of trends (underreaction): anchoring, the disposition effect (sell winners early, hold losers), central banks smoothing FX/rates, mechanical rebalancers (60/40 sells stocks after outperformance), slow-moving capital. Mechanisms for continuation (delayed overreaction): herding, confirmation bias, fund flows chasing performance, and forced trend-following risk management (stop-losses, portfolio insurance, corporate hedging after prices move: airlines buying oil after a rise). End: 3-5 year moves partly reverse (value/long-run reversal).
- 12.2 Method (pp. 212-214): 58 liquid instruments (24 commodity futures, 9 equity index futures, 13 bond futures, 12 currency forwards), Jan 1985 - Jun 2012. Signal = sign of past excess return over 1, 3 or 12 months; always long or short. Position size = 40% / ex ante annualised vol (exponentially weighted squared returns), so each market has equal risk; weekly rebalance at Friday close on data up to Thursday; gross of costs. Average single-market gross Sharpe: 0.29 (1M), 0.36 (3M), 0.38 (12M); positive in almost every market.
- 12.3 Diversified results (Table 12.1, p.218; scaled to 10% vol): by asset class: commodities Sharpe 1.05, equities 0.78, fixed income 1.00, currencies 0.87, all 1.79 (excess return 19.4%, vol 10.8%). By horizon: 1M 1.26, 3M 1.43, 12M 1.52, all 1.79. Alphas vs MSCI World, Barclays bond, GSCI: 17.4%, t = 8.42 (p.218). Gross of costs and fees.
- 12.4 Diversification (pp. 219-221): average pairwise correlation of single-market strategies below 0.1; correlation of diversified TSMOM with S&P 500 -0.02, bonds 0.23, GSCI 0.05. Quarterly return vs S&P 500 forms a "smile": best in big bear and big bull quarters, weaker in flat markets. Fails when a long book meets a sudden crash.
- 12.5 Explaining CTAs (pp. 221-223): managed futures indices and the five largest managers: Sharpe 0.27-0.88 after fees (BTOP 50: 0.50, DJCS 0.27); regressing them on 1M/3M/12M TSMOM gives R2 0.46-0.64, correlation to diversified TSMOM 0.66-0.78, and alphas mostly negative after controlling for TSMOM. A 2-and-20 fee costs about 6%/yr on the strategy at 10% vol; transaction costs 1-4%/yr for a sophisticated trader (more for others, more historically); after both, Sharpe about 1. The author admits hindsight in choosing the 1/3/12-month horizons.
- 12.6 Implementation (pp. 224-225): daily and weekly rebalancing give similar gross Sharpe, monthly and quarterly trail, with short horizons losing the most (Fig 12.5). Costs rise with rebalance frequency and are higher for short signals, so large managers weight medium and long trends. Margin 8-12% for large institutions, over double for small ones. Constant-vol sizing is the risk control; some managers add drawdown control and counter-trend modules.
- 12.7 Harding (Winton) interview (pp. 225-229): looks for non-50/50 odds in data; one model type for all markets, because very different models per market invite overfitting; trades slowly so models change only gradually; profits from surprises that unfold gradually; says managed futures is not a tail hedge (CTA equity exposure can be long into a crash: "tail exacerbating"); trend following has few embedded assumptions; more trend followers would change the autocorrelation structure of prices; allocation across asset classes is a research question with imprecise answers. No parameter values given.

### Topic tags
- trend: TSMOM sign-of-return 1/3/12 months, vol-scaled, 58 futures, gross Sharpe 1.8 diversified (EI p.218); Harding: one model for all markets; trends arise from underreaction, then herding, and are partly reversed after 3-5 years (p.210-212).
- breakout: not covered.
- mean-reversion: 5-year reversal as value proxy in currencies and commodities (p.197); 60/40 rebalancing as a contrarian flow (p.169); Soros: bubbles end in reversal, timing unknowable.
- momentum/oscillators: cross-sectional macro momentum across country indices, currencies, bonds, commodities works, negatively correlated with value (p.198-199).
- patterns: none; Soros's seven-stage bubble is narrative, not testable as stated.
- intraday/sessions: none.
- seasonality/time: Taylor-rule rate path and FOMC days mentioned only as macro anecdotes (p.190-191).
- volatility: constant-vol sizing at 40% per asset (10% portfolio) with exponentially weighted vol; Sharpe-driven risk scaling (p.171, 213); high volatility forces liquidity preference (Soros p.204).
- stops-and-exits: no stops in TSMOM (sign flips are the exit); stop-losses of others feed trends (p.212); some managers use drawdown control (p.225).
- testing-and-robustness/overfitting: dividend-yield timing in-sample is flagged as cheating, with Welch-Goyal out-of-sample failure (p.175); TSMOM horizons chosen with hindsight (p.221); Harding: separate per-market models overfit (p.227); alpha of CTAs vs TSMOM turns negative (p.223).
- costs/execution: fees about 6%/yr (2 and 20) and transaction costs 1-4%/yr bring TSMOM Sharpe from 1.8 to about 1 (p.223-224); weekly equals daily rebalancing, monthly worse (p.224).
- psychology/discipline: disposition effect and herding as trend causes (p.211); Soros: constantly re-examine the hypothesis (p.207).
- portfolio/diversification: equal-risk weighting across 58 markets; pairwise correlation below 0.1 gives most of the Sharpe lift from 0.3-0.4 per market to 1.8 (p.219); carry across four asset classes Sharpe 1.4 vs 0.5-0.9 each (p.188); risk parity.

### Testable ideas
(Mechanism = who is forced or slow; every idea here is daily or weekly, not intraday.)

1. ★ **Vol-scaled TSMOM on a broad futures/CFD set with a cost-first filter** - Rule: weekly (Friday close, signal known Thursday) sign of the past 12-month (also 3M, 1M) excess return, long or short, size = target vol / EWMA vol per instrument, equal risk; no stops. Test on futures-like instruments we have (EIA energy contracts 1-4 continuation, Yahoo 10-year Treasury continuous, VIX futures excluded) and on FTMO CFD indices, FX, gold, oil using daily bars; horizon grid fixed beforehand to {1,3,12} months only. Source: Ch 12 pp. 212-218. Evidence: gross Sharpe 0.29-0.38 per market, 1.79 diversified, 1985-2012; after fees and costs about 1. Fit: HIGH cost sensitivity at weekly turnover is small on liquid index CFDs (spread plus swap on overnight holds, swap is the key cost for CFDs held weeks); decent on futures without swap. Mechanism: slow capital, central-bank smoothing, forced trend flows (stop-losses, hedging). Difference: our failed "CFD trend following" used few instruments and price-pattern entries; this is the diversified vol-scaled version, so mostly a power and diversification check. Expect low power with about 30 markets and few years; use as a reference benchmark for new ideas, honest verdict likely "unproven-positive" at best.
2. ★ **Cross-asset carry sort** - Rule: monthly rank instruments within asset class by carry (currency: swap-based interest differential from CFD swap rates; commodity: slope between EIA contract 1 and contract 2 or 3; bond: Treasury yield minus financing from FRED; equity: dividend yield proxy); long top, short bottom, vol-scaled, equal risk across classes. Source: Ch 11.1, p.185-188, Table 11.1. Evidence: Sharpe 0.6 currency, 0.8 bond, 0.5 commodity, 0.9 equity, 1.4 diversified, 1980s-2011, before costs. Fit: GOOD for commodity roll-slope on EIA contracts 1-4 (we have real per-contract prices) and for crypto perps (funding rate is the exact carry; related to Strategy 20 but time-series carry, not taker-flow); POOR for FX CFDs, because the CFD swap is the carry and is charged at spread-like levels (swap eats the carry). Mechanism: hedgers pay convenience yield; leverage-constrained investors overpay for risky high-carry assets; carry unwinds are priced as crash risk. Difference: GSCI roll was a roll-period test; this is a curve-slope carry on a position held a month, not a roll-window effect.
3. ★ **Crypto perp funding carry (time-series, not flow-sorted)** - Rule: weekly, long perps with the lowest/negative funding, short perps with the highest funding (or only long-short market-neutral across the cross-section), vol-scaled, hold one week. Source: Ch 11.1 carry definition (carry = payoff if prices unchanged) applied to perps, p.187. Evidence: indirect (carry Sharpe 0.5-0.9 in other classes). Fit: GOOD because funding data are in hand, costs are modelled, and the counterparty (leveraged longs who must pay funding) is forced. Difference: Strategy 20 sorts on taker flow; this sorts on funding itself, so treat as a separate ledger entry and check correlation with Strategy 20 before counting it as new.
4. ★ **Value + momentum combo across country equity indices and currencies** - Rule: monthly, for each index/FX pair compute (a) 12-month return momentum and (b) long-run reversal (negative 5-year return, or for indices P/B-like value if available; otherwise 5-year price return as the value proxy); z-score each, average, long top third and short bottom third, vol-scaled. Source: Ch 11.4, p.197-199. Evidence: both signals work in four asset classes; correlation -0.35 to -0.46 between value and momentum; combo strongest (book gives figure only, no Sharpe). Fit: MODERATE; we have about 10 years of MT5 daily data for index CFDs (5-year reversal needs longer histories, which we lack for most CFDs; US shares daily from 2015 only). Difference: cross-sectional macro sort with negative-correlated legs, not our single-instrument trend tests. Likely underpowered; best used as a method to define a 12M-momentum plus 3M-reversal pair, power check first.
5. **Dividend-yield equity timing as a hand-made out-of-sample test** - Rule: monthly, hold long the US equity index (or SPY via Alpaca) when trailing D/P is above its expanding-window median, else cash. Source: Ch 10, p.173-175 (regression t = 2.8, 11.2% vs 4.2%). Evidence: in-sample 1926-2013; Welch-Goyal say it fails out of sample. Fit: POOR for our budget (one asset, few independent years, expanding median needs history); use only as an illustration of an expanding-window protocol.
6. **Stagflation/overheating regime tilt (growth x inflation)** - Rule: classify monthly into four regimes from FRED growth/inflation data and hold the book of bonds/stocks/gold implied by Table 11.2-11.3. Source: Ch 11.3, p.191-196. Evidence: none quantified. Fit: POOR; too many regimes for our sample length, high risk of fitting; listed only so it can be rejected on power grounds.
7. **Risk parity benchmark** - Rule: weights inversely proportional to trailing vol across equity index, Treasury futures, gold and oil, levered to a target vol; monthly rebalance. Source: p.171. Evidence: argues higher risk-adjusted return historically; no number given. Fit: GOOD as a baseline for portfolio construction (daily Yahoo Treasury futures plus index and gold CFD data), not as an alpha idea; cost is financing/swap on leverage.
8. **Trend-follower pressure tests: stop-loss/hedger flows after large moves** - Rule: after a 3-month vol-scaled price move above 2 sigma in an energy or index contract, test whether the following 1-4 weeks drift continues more than it does after smaller moves (nonlinear TSMOM). Source: p.211-212 (forced trend-following flows) and footnote 6 (position size rising with trend strength). Evidence: none given. Fit: GOOD for EIA energy contracts 1-4 where hedging flows are plausible; differs from simple trend following by conditioning on move size and by being tested as a drift in a forced-flow window.

### Warnings / pitfalls
- Timing the market is a single bet at near even odds; the dividend-yield predictor looks good only in-sample and most predictors fail out of sample (p.175). Choose the predictor, threshold and coefficient before the data.
- TSMOM results are gross of costs; fees (about 6%/yr at 2-and-20) and transaction costs (1-4%/yr, more historically) take the Sharpe from 1.8 to about 1; actual manager alphas turn negative once TSMOM is controlled (p.223).
- The 1/3/12-month horizons carry hindsight; real managers also had to live through drawdowns (p.221).
- Carry strategies have crash risk (stairs up, elevator down); diversification across currencies does not remove it; pegged currencies look like arbitrage until the peg breaks (p.185-187). Leveraging carry multiplies the tail loss.
- Trend following is not a guaranteed tail hedge; long exposure into a fast crash gives losses (Harding, p.228; Ch 12.4).
- Short horizon signals (1M) are the most cost- and rebalance-frequency-sensitive; monthly or quarterly rebalancing destroys part of the edge (p.224).
- Harding: market-specific models invite overfitting; research gives imprecise answers; there are no final truths, strategies decay (p.227-229).
- Soros: timing of bubble turning points is unknowable; he lost money shorting early in 2000 (p.206). Discretionary themes (Ch 11.5) have no testable rule.
- Persistent predictors bias t-stats (Stambaugh footnote, p.173); overlapping returns need adjusted standard errors.

# Part 4: Part IV, ch.13-16: arbitrage, fixed income, convertibles, event-driven


### Overview
Part IV covers "arbitrage" in the practitioner sense: trades that buy and sell nearly the same thing at attractive relative prices. They are never risk free. They need cash for margin, can lose before they converge, and the link between the two legs can break. The book's thesis is that the profit is pay for liquidity risk and deal risk, so prices stay "efficiently inefficient": the spread is just wide enough to pay the arbitrageur for bearing the risk (EI p.233-234). Chapter 13 gives the pricing framework and option parity. Chapter 14 covers fixed income (yield curve, carry, on-the-run vs off-the-run, swap spreads, credit, mortgages) with a Scholes interview. Chapter 15 covers convertible bonds (hedged long convertible, short stock) with a Griffin interview. Chapter 16 covers event-driven investing (merger arbitrage, spin-offs, carve-outs, distress, capital structure) with a Paulson interview. The author's stance: the edge comes from a counterparty that must or wants to trade for non-price reasons (clienteles, issuers needing cash, shareholders fleeing deal risk, index funds). Leverage and funding are the main danger. Very little here is backtestable on our data directly (no bond, option or deal data), but the mechanisms and the sizing and risk lessons transfer. Almost no hard numbers beyond Mitchell-Pulvino merger arbitrage.

### Chapter notes

### Ch 13 - Introduction to Arbitrage Pricing and Trading (pp. 233-241)
- Textbook arbitrage (no cash, positive profit with positive probability) almost never exists. Practitioner arbitrage needs margin, can show losses before convergence, and can break (p.233).
- Three kinds of arbitrage pricing, with rising frictions: (1) same payoff = same price, (2) replicating portfolio, (3) self-financing dynamic hedge. Type 3 needs constant trading and breaks down most easily when costs are high (p.235).
- Arbitrage is strongest when there is a natural convergence time (option expiry, bond maturity). Non-fungible instruments, such as dual-listed shares that cannot be swapped between exchanges, have no convergence time and can stay mispriced for long (p.235).
- Put-call parity (Ct - Pt + PV(X) = St) holds well because it is buy-and-hold with fixed expiry. It breaks when the stock is hard to short (p.236).
- Binomial tree and Black-Scholes-Merton: price an option as a replicating hedge, delta rebalanced at least daily. Implied volatility is the comparison measure. Short options whose implied vol is above your own vol forecast. The index put smirk reflects real crash risk, not only an arbitrage (pp.236-240).
- Demand-based option pricing: investors want crash insurance and embedded leverage, intermediaries face costs, so demand pushes option prices. Banks and hedge funds who sell the insurance earn an expected (not certain) profit (p.241; refs Bollen-Whaley 2004, Garleanu-Pedersen-Poteshman 2009).

### Ch 14 - Fixed-Income Arbitrage (pp. 241-268)
- Saying: "picking up nickels in front of a steamroller". Relative-value spreads are small, so leverage is high. Many arbitrageurs delever together in a fire sale, causing rare large losses (p.241).
- Bond math: return over one period is about YTM minus modified duration times yield change (eq. 14.7). Leveraged bond return is YTM minus repo minus duration times yield change (14.9). Duration-matched ("immunized") long/short hedges level shifts only. Convexity helps both sides of a yield move (pp.243-247).
- Forward rate = break-even rate priced in by the curve. Only bet when you expect yields to move more than the forward implies (pp.247-248).
- Yield = expected average short rate + risk premium. The expectations hypothesis (risk premium zero and constant) is rejected: term premium is positive on average and varies with carry, risk appetite, pension hedging demand, official buyers and supply ("preferred habitat") (pp.248-249). Footnote 4: Sharpe ratios are highest for short maturities because unleveraged long bonds are preferred by investors, so short bonds need leverage and must pay more per unit of risk (p.249).
- Level, slope and curvature trades. Slope: steepener (long 2y, short 10y) sized duration neutral, example hedge ratio x = 0.23 of 10y per 2y bond for durations 1.9 and 8.1 (p.251). Some traders vol-match instead of duration-match because long yields move less than short yields. Butterfly: short the "rich" center bond (e.g. pension funds buying it), long left and right wings with D_L + D_R = D_C and slope betas b, g (example weights 0.5/0.5 for b = 0.9, g = 1.1). Remaining risk is the "kink" widening further (pp.250-255).
- Pricing error versus trading opportunity: you know it was an opportunity only if it made money, but you gain confidence if you can name a price-insensitive buyer (insurers, pension funds) and by backtesting the signal systematically (pp.251-252).
- Carry = yield plus roll-down (price gain from ageing along an unchanged curve); exact carry is the forward rate (p.255, fn 6). Example fall 2013: 10y yield 2.6% plus roll-down 1.6% (duration 8 times 0.20 pp slope between 9y and 10y) = 4.2% carry versus short rate under 0.25%. High carry predicts high bond returns (Koijen-Moskowitz-Pedersen-Vrugt 2012). Because the EH fails, carry is a usable signal (p.256).
- On-the-run vs off-the-run: new Treasuries are liquid and cheap to finance (low repo), older ones are cheaper with higher yield. The trade buys off-the-run and shorts on-the-run when the spread has blown out; P&L = yield difference minus financing spread plus convergence term (14.25). Spread rose in the 1998 LTCM crisis and 2008-09 and was tiny in early 2007 when arbitrage capital was plentiful; fig. 14.10 shows the 10-year spread peaking near 0.8 pp (pp.257-259). The reverse trade is a bet on a liquidity crisis.
- Swaps and swap spreads: receiver swap is like a leveraged long bond. Swap spread = swap rate minus Treasury yield, positive because LIBOR (unsecured) is above repo (secured). Typical "tightener" (long swap, short leveraged Treasury) earns positive carry when the spread exceeds the average LIBOR-repo financing spread (pp.259-260).
- Credit: credit spread = default probability times loss rate plus risk premium. Buy bonds with spread wide relative to default risk, hedge rates with government bonds or swaps, diversify firm-specific risk. Relative-value trades: CDS-bond basis, CDX index vs constituents, capital structure arbitrage using the Merton model (pp.260-261).
- Mortgages: MBS basis trade (long MBS, short Treasuries or swaps), paid for prepayment (negative convexity), liquidity and some credit risk. TBA futures and tranche relative-value (p.261).
- Rates vol, municipal spreads, bond futures basis (cheapest to deliver), break-even inflation (p.262).
- Interview with Myron Scholes (pp.262-268), the key mechanism material:
  - Fixed-income arbitrage "intermediates" supply and demand imbalances between segmented clienteles: insurers and pension funds at the long end, macro funds at the 10-year, mortgage issuers at 10-15y, banks and corporates at the short end. Opportunities are mostly mean-reverting; what matters is why the price deviates, how fast it reverts, and how much capital it ties up (pp.263-264).
  - Four trade families: curve, spread, convexity, basis.
  - Curve example: macro funds buy Japanese bond futures; dealers hedge with the cheapest-to-deliver (say 7-year), which makes the 7-year rich versus a mix of 5y and 10y. The curve trader shorts the 7y and buys 5y/10y (pp.264-265).
  - Pension example: Dutch and other European pension funds, forced to hedge liabilities after equity losses (2002, 2008), received fixed in long-end swaps, driving long-end swap yields too low versus bonds. Trade: sell 30y swaps, buy 10y, hedge slope with 2y (p.265).
  - Convexity examples: mortgage originators who locked rates must hedge the option they wrote when refinancing waves hit; servicers hedge shortened fee streams when rates fall. Intermediaries sell the convexity protection (pp.265-266).
  - Rule: if you cannot identify the flow, hesitate. Wait until the deviation is large ("a nickel cheap: stay out; a dollar cheap: go in") because flows may continue against you (momentum effect) and you add if they do. Returns on capital must be high enough (pp.266-267).
  - Broker-dealers tell the arbitrageur why they want to offload risk; it is like reinsurance of the dealer community (p.267).
  - Why spreads widen in stress: capital and human attention are scarce, finance runs on "volatility time"; intermediaries become demanders of liquidity and withdraw capital (pp.267-268).
  - Closing lesson: capital structure (leverage, debt duration, investor trust) matters as much as the trade; a long-only structure with securities borrowing may be a better vehicle than a leveraged fund (p.268).

### Ch 15 - Convertible Bond Arbitrage (pp. 269-291)
- Convertible = straight bond plus call option on the issuer's stock. Terms: conversion ratio = par / conversion price; parity value = ratio times stock price. Issuers are often small firms needing cash fast; deals are often 144a (restricted) and illiquid for 3-6 months until registered; they are sold at a discount (like IPO underpricing). Part of the arbitrageur's profit is simply getting allocations in the primary market (pp.269-270).
- Trade: buy the cheap convertible, short delta shares of stock, hedge rates/credit at portfolio level, collect coupons, pay dividends and borrow fees, rehedge as stock moves. The trade is almost always long convertible (convertibles have been cheap as a liquidity premium); reversed trades are rare (pp.270-271).
- Why it is cheap (mechanism): issuers need cash quickly and hedge funds are the main marginal buyer, so the discount depends on hedge-fund capital and leverage versus supply. When funds face redemptions or prime brokers pull financing, converts get very cheap (pp.270-271, 281).
- Valuation: bond floor + option; solved with trees. Delta hedge ratio rises toward the conversion ratio as the bond goes in the money (pp.272-275).
- "Never convert" rule has exceptions: before dividends, impending merger that hurts the bond, high stock borrow fees, and funding costs for deep-in-the-money bonds (pp.275-276; Jensen-Pedersen 2012).
- P&L drivers: positive gamma (profit from large moves either way: example stock 55 to 85 to 55 gives +97.17 and +57.99 = +155.16 on one bond with delta rehedged from 13.4 to 18.6 shares, table 15.1), negative theta (time decay when nothing moves), positive vega, and "alpha" = buying below theoretical value (pp.277-282).
- Types: distressed, busted, hybrid, high-moneyness (equity sensitive, needs most leverage) (pp.282-283).
- Unhedgeable risk: systematic cheapening of converts, funding liquidity, market liquidity, which tend to arrive together. Events: 1998 (LTCM), 2005, 2008. In 2008 prime-broker leverage on a large fund fell sharply (fig 15.9) and busted converts' yield advantage over the same issuer's straight bonds fell from above 6% to near zero or negative (596-bond sample, fig 15.10), because converts were held by leveraged funds and straights by unleveraged long-only investors (pp.283-285). In 1998 prices fell versus theoretical value, then the hedge-fund index earned high returns as the cheapness reversed (fig 15.11, p.285-286).
- Event risks: takeover below conversion price can hit both legs (bond falls to par or below, hedge stock jumps). Takeover-protection put clauses became common (p.285-286).
- Interview with Ken Griffin (pp.286-290): started 1987 with about $265,000 from friends and family, used the S&P bond guide to spot cheap converts. Many mispricings existed where the stock was hard to borrow; being small let him borrow quantities too small for big funds, via retail brokers such as Schwab. Dealers called him with hard-to-borrow names. Stayed over-hedged in the 1987 crash. Lesson: good businesses sit "where liquidity exists" - liquid enough to take size, but where you understand value better than others; diversify across strategies since each is cyclical (pp.287-290).

### Ch 16 - Event-Driven Investments (pp. 291-322)
- Event-driven classes: merger arbitrage; spin-offs, split-offs, carve-outs; capital-structure changes; distressed; capital-structure arbitrage; special structures (ETFs, closed-end funds, SPACs, PIPEs); market-structure events such as index inclusion/exclusion. Two principles: isolate the event risk and hedge market, rate and credit risk; diversify across many independent events. Buffett quote on casino-style diversification (pp.291-292).
- Merger arbitrage mechanism: the target price jumps after the bid but not all the way to the offer. Many original holders sell because they do not want deal-completion risk; they accept the deal spread as the price of "insurance". Arbitrageurs buy and provide that liquidity. Deal spread = (offer - price) / price (example offer 130, target at 120: 8.3%). Spreads are wider when merger capital is small relative to deals; riskier deals have wider spreads (pp.293-296).
- Spread path (fig 16.3, Mitchell-Pulvino 2001): successful deals start near 8% and shrink as obstacles clear; failing deals start near 20% and gap to around 30% at failure (pp.297-298).
- Hedge by deal type: cash deal, buy target only; fixed exchange ratio, short the acquirer at the ratio (e.g. 1.2 shares); floating ratio, no hedge until the pricing period, then ramp up; collar deals, use options or delta hedge (pp.300-303). Acquirers in fixed-ratio deals issue more press releases during negotiation (Ahern-Sosyura) (p.303).
- Portfolio: about 251 US deals per year (1963-98), average duration about 3 months, so about 63 deals live at once (over 100 in the 2000s). Position cap 3-10% of NAV. Example: 5% position, target rose 20% then falls back 20%, loss is 1% of capital. About 10% of deals fail. Failure is more likely for hostile deals, LBOs, small deals, regulatory risk, and in falling markets; best simple predictor of failure is the spread itself (pp.303-305).
- Evidence (Mitchell-Pulvino RAIM index, 1963-98, all cash and stock deals, 10% NAV cap and 5% impact cap, after costs): arithmetic return 11.1% (geometric 10.64%) per year, volatility 7.74%, Sharpe 0.63 (0.57 geometric) versus market Sharpe 0.40 same period. Beta near zero in flat and up markets, positive in market falls over 5% a month (short-put-like payoff), so alpha must be measured against both the market and a short put position; alpha stays significantly positive (pp.305-307).
- Portfolio hedges (short index futures or buy puts) are usually skipped as too expensive (p.307).
- Spin-offs: the subsidiary is often sold by index and mandate-driven holders (different industry, not in the parent's index, little analyst coverage), then recovers medium term as focus improves. Spin-offs face more selling pressure than split-offs. Carve-outs: "stub value" = parent equity minus stake in subsidiary; negative stub value is a true arbitrage only if the stake will be distributed. Example 3Com/Palm 2000 (fig 16.10). Risks: tax approval, short borrow impossible or recalled, wild stub swings; Mitchell-Pulvino-Stafford (2002): in 82 negative-stub cases, in 30% the link was cut before the gap closed; returns would be 50% higher with smooth convergence (pp.308-311).
- Distressed: passive distressed-debt index returns were disappointing; value comes from active involvement (creditor committees, DIP financing). Price pressure on downgrade to junk or bankruptcy is liquidity provision (pp.311-312). Capital-structure changes: buyback signals confidence, equity issues overvaluation; participating in offerings is profitable if you get allocations in even the hot ones; rights offerings, when-issued markets, ETFs and closed-end funds at NAV discounts (pp.312-313).
- Index inclusion: historically a predictable price rise around inclusion as index funds buy, "not strong anymore"; deletion drops (p.313).
- Interview with John Paulson (pp.313-322):
  - Merger arbitrage rule of thumb: stock at 30, bid 50, stock goes to 49; make 1 on the upside, risk 19. $1 on $49 in 2 months is about 2% or roughly 12% annualized. Sellers are unhedged long-term holders who do not understand antitrust or the merger agreement; he reads agreement, financing, regulatory approvals (pp.315-316).
  - Topping-bid screening: compute deal multiples (earnings, EBITDA, EBIT) and compare with a database of sector deals; if the price is low versus comps and the sale was a private negotiation (not an auction) another bidder is likely. Check breakup fees and lock-ups. Then size up to the 10% cap (of his portfolio and of the company). Sprint example: bought 220 million shares near $5.75 (about $1.3bn); Dish bid 7, SoftBank raised to 7.75, profit about $440 million (pp.316-318).
  - Anticipate targets by industry consolidation logic (small telecom players); Leap Wireless stake 9.9% paid a 110% premium. Explicitly avoids inside information (p.318-319).
  - Bankruptcy investing: Extended Stay Hotels, bought debt, pooled with another fund to hold one-third (veto over a plan needing two-thirds), bought the company out of bankruptcy, $1.5bn equity grew to $6bn (pp.319-320).
  - Subprime short: BBB tranche only 1% thick, wiped out by about 6% pool loss; net carry cost about 1% a year versus a roughly 100% payoff; sized up from $100m to $25bn notional, kept the position as losses became inevitable (pp.321-322). Lesson: size by maximum loss, not by notional; "first think about the downside" (p.318).

### Topic tags
- trend: Scholes notes trend following competes with mean reversion; arbitrageurs add to positions when flows keep going against them (momentum effect) (p.266-267). Not a rule, only a caution.
- breakout: nothing.
- mean-reversion: most fixed-income relative-value trades are mean-reverting spreads; entry only when the spread is large enough to pay for the risk and capital ("a nickel cheap: wait") (pp.263-267). On-the-run/off-the-run, swap spread, butterfly kink, convertible cheapness, stub value, merger spread all converge to a known date or to fundamental value.
- momentum/oscillators: carry as a predictor of bond returns (Koijen et al.) (p.256). No oscillators.
- patterns: none (pure relative value).
- intraday/sessions: none. Merger announcements arrive when the market is closed or the stock is halted (p.296).
- seasonality/time: time to convergence (3-month merger duration, option expiry, bond maturity) decides how much capital the trade ties up; annualize deal spreads (2% in 2 months = 12%) (p.315).
- volatility: convertible arbitrage is long gamma and vega, short theta (pp.277-281); implied versus forecast volatility as option arbitrage signal (p.239); "volatility time" explains why spreads widen in stress (p.267).
- stops-and-exits: no stops; trades end by convergence, event, or forced margin liquidation. Risk is controlled by diversification and position caps, not stop orders.
- position-sizing/risk: cap any deal at 3-10% of NAV; loss on a failed deal = position times drop (5% times 20% = 1%) (pp.303-304); size by loss, not by gain (Paulson p.318); leverage and funding capacity set unhedgeable risk (fig 15.9).
- testing-and-robustness/overfitting: author warns pricing-error versus opportunity can only be judged by whether it makes money, so use flow knowledge plus systematic backtests (p.252); merger arbitrage alpha must be tested against a short-put factor, not just CAPM beta (pp.306-307); returns are non-linear (short crash risk), so Sharpe understates tail risk.
- costs/execution: arbitrage trades pay costs, funding, and short fees; costs for dynamic hedging (type 3) are high (p.235); convertible short fees can force early conversion (p.276); merger index returns are after brokerage and market impact (p.305).
- psychology/discipline: Paulson on staying in a trade when the mark-to-market gain is large but the fundamental payoff is not yet reached (p.322); Griffin on being the specialist where liquidity exists (p.290).
- portfolio/diversification: many small independent event bets (about 60-100 deals live), non-linear market exposure in crashes (pp.303-307), Griffin on running many strategies since each is cyclical (p.290).

### Testable ideas
(Most need data we do not have. Items that can be tried on our data are marked and ranked.)

1. **Carry / roll-down on Treasury futures or yield data** - rule: each week rank countries or maturities by carry (yield plus roll-down using FRED yields: 10y yield plus 8 times the 9y-10y slope), long high-carry, short low-carry, or scale a single 10y long by carry minus short rate. Hold weekly. Source: Ch 14, section 14.4, p.255-256, KMPV 2012. Author's claim: carry predicts bond returns; example 4.2% vs 0.25% in 2013. Fit: moderate. We have FRED yields and continuous 10y futures only, one country (US), so cross-country is impossible; time-series carry on one bond is a single stream with long swings. Differs from CFD trend: it is a risk-premium timing signal, not price pattern. Unproven; the month-end duration stream we already passed overlaps (same asset).
2. ★ **Merger-spread style deal-risk liquidity premium on US shares** - rule: from Alpaca daily bars, detect targets of cash buyouts by a one-day jump above 15% followed by a very low-volatility, price-pinned path (flag via rolling 20-day volatility under a small multiple of market volatility); buy after day 2, hold 60 trading days or until the price jumps/drops out of the pinned band, equal weight, cap 3-5% per name, many names at once. Source: Ch 16, 16.1, pp.294-307. Evidence: RAIM 11.1% a year, Sharpe 0.63, 1963-98, after costs; roughly 10% of deals fail. Fit: good as a research idea (counterparty that must trade: holders who sell deal risk), poor on data purity, because we have no deal terms and no delisting or acquirer data; pinned stocks that later delist may be missing from Alpaca (survivorship). Differs: event liquidity provision, not price pattern. Needs a pre-registered way to avoid lookahead (the pin must be observable at entry) and a check of what happens to delisted names in the data.
3. ★ **Spin-off forced-selling reversal** - rule: for spun-off shares (identify from new tickers with a parent ticker drop on the same date, or from a listing of spin-offs), go long the subsidiary after the first 5 days (selling pressure from index and mandate mismatch) and hold 3-6 months, hedged by short of the parent or an index; alternative: long the parent-stub. Source: pp.308-309. Author claims selling pressure then focus gains but gives no numbers. Fit: depends on whether Alpaca covers new tickers and survivors; small samples per year (maybe dozens); needs t >= 3 with few events, so a power check first. Differs: forced seller (index and mandate selling), not price pattern.
4. ★ **Index inclusion / deletion event study on Alpaca shares** - rule: long stocks announced for S&P 500 inclusion between announcement and effective date (or short deletions), exit at effective close. Source: p.313. Author says the effect was predictable but "not strong anymore". Fit: poor to unknown (needs announcement dates, not in our data; the S&P publishes them but we do not hold them); the author already says it faded. Mention as a family check only; month-end index flow (our spent test) is the same counterparty type.
5. ★ **Cross-sectional dealer / flow-imbalance rule: curve butterfly mean reversion on Treasury yields** - rule: fit a level/slope/curvature model on FRED 2y, 5y, 7y, 10y, 30y yields; when the 7y (or 5y) residual vs a 2y-10y-30y fit exceeds a z-score threshold (e.g. 2 on a 60-day window), trade the butterfly in proxy futures or ETFs, duration-neutral. Source: Ch 14, 14.3 and Scholes, pp.251-255, 264-265. Author's claim: price-insensitive buyers (pension, dealers hedging futures) make wings or belly rich and the deviation reverts; no numbers. Fit: poor on execution (we have yields not tradeable instruments except 10y continuous futures; rolling cost and bid-ask on proxies unknown), but a pre-registered test on yields alone could show whether the effect exists at all. Differs: a relative-value spread with an identified flow, not trend following.
6. **On-the-run vs off-the-run, swap spread** - rule: long the cheaper leg when the spread exceeds a high percentile of its history. Source: pp.257-260. Fit: none on our data (no off-the-run prices, no swap rates).
7. **Convertible bond cheapness** - rule: long cheap converts, short delta shares. Source: Ch 15. Fit: none (no convertible data). Possible only as a risk analogue: the funding-crisis pattern (cheapness peaks in forced deleveraging) can be tested on our own spreads, such as VIX basis or Treasury yield spreads, around known stress dates.
8. **Negative stub value (parent vs subsidiary)** - rule: buy parent, short subsidiary share times the holding ratio when the stub is negative. Source: pp.309-311. Fit: poor; needs holdings and corporate action data, rare, and borrow can fail (30% of cases cut before convergence).
9. **Options: sell implied vol when implied is above own forecast, demand-based pricing** - Source: pp.239-241. Fit: poor on our data; we have VIX futures only. Differs: our VIX basis test is spent.
10. **Pension liability-hedging demand as a flow signal** - rule: after large equity falls (for example drops of more than 10% over 3 months) go long long-dated Treasuries (or the 30y part of the curve) because forced pension hedging buys the long end; Scholes cites 2002 and 2008. Source: p.265. Fit: testable on daily data with yields and our US equity index CFDs, daily or weekly; thin sample of events (2002, 2008, 2020, 2022 as counter-example). A flow-driven "forced buyer" idea, but flights to quality confound it; needs a causal control (e.g. against changes in the short rate).

### Warnings / pitfalls
- Arbitrage trades are not riskless: losses can come before convergence, the link can break, margin is needed (p.233). Convertible and fixed-income arbitrage lose together in liquidity crises (1998, 2005, 2008), when funding, market liquidity and cheapness move against the trade at once (pp.283-285, 267-268). Backtests that ignore leverage constraints overstate what survives.
- Merger spreads reflect real deal risk, so a positive spread does not mean positive expected profit; historical profit is a liquidity premium, and returns are non-linear: near zero beta in normal times, loss in market falls (short put payoff) (pp.294-295, 305-307). Sharpe ratios hide this; use put-augmented regressions.
- Mitchell-Pulvino index is built after costs and market impact but on hypothetical and well-diversified positions; individual deals can lose 15-20 points on failure (p.315, 304).
- Negative stub arbitrage failed to converge in 30% of 82 cases; returns would have been 50% higher with smooth paths (p.311). Shorting hard-to-borrow stock can be impossible or recalled (p.310-311, 288).
- Decision of "pricing error versus opportunity" is only known after the fact; confirm with an identified flow (pp.251-252, Scholes p.266).
- Index inclusion effect has faded; do not assume old edges survive (p.313).
- Passive distressed-debt investing has historically not paid well; only active involvement does (p.311).
- Scholes: flows can continue against you before reverting; size entries to be able to add; high leverage in the capital structure is the biggest danger (pp.266-268).
- Paulson: size by what you can lose; cap concentrated deals at 10% (pp.317-318).
- Griffin: small size was an advantage in hard-to-borrow converts; the edge is capacity-limited and shrinks with scale (p.289).
