# Kaufman, Trading Systems and Methods 5th ed., Ch 21-24 + appendices (book pp. 905-1145)

## Overview
- Covers how to TEST (Ch21), what goes wrong between test and live (Ch22), how to measure/control RISK and size positions (Ch23), and how to ALLOCATE across markets/systems (Ch24). Appendices 1-3 (stat tables, matrix solving/Markov chains, trig regression, pp. 1147-1172) are pure math reference, nothing usable.
- Stance: testing is for VALIDATING a pre-formed idea with a sound premise, never for discovery. The best optimisation result never lives up; the realistic expectation is the AVERAGE of all tests over a sensible, pre-declared parameter range. "Begin simply, increase complexity slowly, stop as soon as possible."
- Core enemies: overfitting, price shocks (history flatters you on them), costs/slippage, regime decay, undercapitalisation/leverage. Being out of the market is the only defence against shocks.
- Risk side: size by volatility (equal risk per trade), target a portfolio volatility, cap leverage, never risk more than ~5% per trade (1-3% common), treat max historic drawdown as a floor on future drawdown.
- Portfolio side: diversify across unrelated markets AND unrelated strategy types; correlations go to 1 in a crisis; standard mean-variance fails for intermittent trade returns (his GA alternative uses drawdown semivariance as risk).

## Chapter notes

### Ch 21 - System Testing (pp. 905-981)
- Expectations first (p.907): write down before testing the expected trade count, % winners, rough risk, timeframe. If results differ from plan, something is wrong (or rules mis-specified). Be as suspicious of GOOD results as of bad; good results for the wrong reason are still an error.
- Success objective must be a blend, not max profit (max profit picks one lucky giant trade). Industry favourite: information ratio = annualised return / annualised st.dev of returns (Sharpe without risk-free), p.907.
- Parameters (pp.908-910): list them up front; limit ranges BEFORE testing (e.g. RSI 3-10, not 3-50). Big grids force a decision about reasonable ranges - an advantage.
- Grid spacing: geometric (percentage) steps, not equal steps (1,2,3,4,5,6,8,10,12,15,20,25,30,40,50 or x1.5 each). Equal steps over-weight the slow end and bias averages.
- Test one parameter at a time, most important first (trend speed), then timing filter, then stops. Stops/risk control refine, they should not rescue a losing core. Continuous/discrete parameters form surfaces; coded (rule-switch) parameters do not.
- Data (pp.910-915): futures continuous-series pitfalls (roll gaps, back-adjusted prices can go negative so percent rules break). Best series = the one matching what you trade. Synthetic data is attractive but unproven; shuffling real data breaks bull/bear sequence (abandoned). (Our spot M1 avoids rolls; point stands: test and trade on the same data source, p.980.)
- In-sample / out-of-sample (pp.916-917): reserve ~50%. Options: first half, last half, alternating blocks, alternating RANDOM blocks (most robust; fix them BEFORE development, never change; different split per system). With alternating blocks, stitch DAILY returns into one stream, not total profit per block. In-sample may be tortured freely; out-of-sample is used ONCE. Typical OOS result: ~50% of in-sample return; information ratio 2.0 -> 1.0 is "reasonably good", 2.0 -> 0.4 is a fail. Fixing anything after seeing OOS = feedback = overfitting.
- Step-forward / walk-forward (pp.918-919): e.g. 20y total, optimise on 2y, trade next 6 months with chosen params, roll 6 months, chain only OOS pieces. Short-window bias: if best parameter flips erratically (10d, then 50d, then 15d) windows are too short -> use 5y. Repeating walk-forward many times uses up OOS data (feedback) -> keep an untouched final hold-out.
- Searching (pp.919-922): best = most robust, not max. Sequential 1-D optimisation (n1+n2+n3 tests instead of product) can miss the peak; use random seeds/starts, repeat until the same peak is found. Genetic algorithm (8 params, 2.3 billion combos, ~30 min) and Monte Carlo sampling of the grid exist. Save top-20 strings: clustered = robust, scattered = suspect.
- Visualising (pp.922-931): 1-D curves (smooth = good; spike surrounded by losses = fluke), 2-D heat maps/surfaces, scatter per parameter. Three 2-D patterns: single broad hill (pick centre), two hills (fast vs slow; pick the slower/wider/cheaper), erratic (strategy does not work, or bug).
- Standardise results (pp.930-931): annualise; risk-adjust; adjust for standard error / trade count (2 trades vs 50 trades are not equal confidence). Smooth surface by replacing each cell with the average of its 3 (1-D) or 9 (2-D) neighbours, then choose the max of the smoothed surface.
- Refining rules (p.937): a new rule must improve the average of ALL tests (or cut dispersion at the same mean). Raises peak but worsens others (higher kurtosis across tests) = fit to one pattern. A rule touching 1-2 trades = overfit.
- Valid results (pp.938-945):
  - Robust system: sound premise (real phenomenon, not mined), adapts (volatility-scaled stops/size; no fixed dollar or price levels), tested on lots of data incl. bull/bear/sideways/shocks. Futures Truth: best systems have 4 or fewer variables.
  - Benchmark against something simple (buy&hold, 60/40).
  - Criteria list (pp.941-942): information ratio; net profit; trade count; % profitable (>60% regular winner; trend system 30-40% is normal); average net trade vs costs (fast systems die from slippage); max drawdown (surprisingly small DD = overfit or tiny sample; use AVERAGE max DD across tests; capitalise ~3x max DD); annualised return; time-to-recovery; time in market (less is better); slope of periodic returns (declining = decay).
  - Judge by AVERAGE of all tests in the pre-declared range; adjusted return = mean of all tests minus 1 st.dev of test returns (~84% chance of achieving).
  - Review outliers: one windfall move (esp. a price shock) must not carry the result; study biggest wins/losses; lots of big wins and no big losses = overfit.
  - Sensitivity test around the chosen point; peak where every neighbour is worse = overfit. n-space version: random parameter sets sorted by distance from core, average performance per distance shell, expect modest decay.
  - "Significance of significant" (p.945): count ALL tests across ALL ideas ever tried; 1 good config in 1000 is not significant. His rule of thumb: ~70% of reasonable-range tests profitable = robust (valid only if the range was not narrowed afterwards).
  - A rule for one market should work on sibling markets (S&P idea should work on Nasdaq/Dow); sibling markets are free out-of-sample.
- Worked comparison (pp.946-949): MA vs regression slope on Nasdaq 1997-2003, periods 10-250 step 10, $125/trade. Most tests profitable (market trends). Profit factor explodes with tiny trade counts (8 trades, PF 76) = meaningless. Judge by average ratio over realistic range (MA PF 2.62 vs slope 2.30 on 10-180).
- Cost realism (p.949): MA on Nasdaq 1991-2011 with 0/50/100/150 per round turn: profitable zone shrinks from ">15 days" to "45-75 days". Costs must be realistic, not extreme.
- Mine the WORST results (pp.950-951): consistently losing fast trend speeds in noisy markets can be used as pullback timing inside a profitable slow trend: buy when slow trend up and fast trend turns down; exit when slow trend turns down. Nasdaq 1991-2011: plain MA net 64k, PF 1.51, 212 trades, 35% wins, 277/trade; with fade net 53k, PF 2.26, 72 trades, 47% wins, 741/trade, no reversals.
- Retesting (pp.951-953): retest after 5-10% new data; parameters should shift only slightly; large shift = too-short original sample or unusual data. After a shock loss do NOT invent a new rule.
- Is the model broken? (p.953): compare drawdown size/duration to the tested profile; a new system that decays from day one is suspect. Probability of drawdown >= P under normal returns (Wolberg) from mean/stdev of daily returns; example: 30%/yr return, mean/stdev = 9 per day... -> 12% drawdown expected in about 5% of months.
- Cross-market testing (pp.954-970): robust = works across many markets with the same rules ("loose pants fit everyone"). His 2011 study: 5 trend methods (MA, exp smoothing, linear-weighted MA, regression slope, N-day breakout), periods 2-80 step 2, 17 futures, 1990-2011, $40 round turn: ALL five average profitable; slower periods better; ~54-65% of tests profitable; breakout/slope best PF but ~2.7 trades per year (hidden risk); non-trending markets (S&P, CAD, gold then, grains) have <50% profitable tests; 11 of 17 above 70% "robustness". Two-MA crossover has lower robustness and profit per contract ($222 vs $357 single MA vs $1301 slope). Lukac/Brorsen/Irwin 1978-84 (12 systems x 12 markets with walk-forward: 3y optimise, next year trade): only a third of systems consistently profitable; rule: don't trade a system profitable in <50% of markets or a market that fails most systems.
- Price shocks in tests (pp.970-975): flag shock days = 1-day range >3.5x recent average (or >5x ATR10); count what share of shocks the system profited from. If >50% profited, profit and risk are both wrong: reverse the average shock-day P/L (just that one day). Also check size distribution of shocks (not small losses and big wins only). (Text is slightly inconsistent on whether shocks added or subtracted in Fig 21.21; the principle is clear: do not let shocks flatter the pick.) Use the largest drawdown among neighbouring parameters, not the lucky one.
- "Anatomy of an optimization" (pp.972-975): crude oil 1990: optimising up to just before each shock picks the exact lookback that happened to be on the right side that day (profit $19.8k with shock vs $3.2k without). Parameter that wins by catching a shock is a fluke.
- Robustness checklist at chapter end (pp.976-981): see "Checklist" below. Extra: trade EXACTLY the tested rules; same data source; monitor live slippage vs system price; "real-time results will not be as good as historic."

### Ch 22 - Practical Considerations (pp. 983-1026)
- Data checks (pp.985-986): outliers >4% jump, missing dates (>4 days), OHLC outside H/L, ticks per interval, reversing spikes; decimal shifts between vendors. If live data is dirtier than test data, the system sees extra signals; use realistic noise.
- Too much power: brute force/NN/GA over everything = overfit; premise first. Use as much history as possible unless structural break (p.988).
- Paper trade (p.988): assume the worst price of the next 5 minutes after an intraday signal; document expectations (frequency, holding period, % winners, win/loss size); start small.
- Vertical vs integrated development (p.989): vertical = core rule profitable alone, then each filter must prove it helps. Integrated (rule works only with filter) accepted only if the % of profitable combos with filter > % without.
- Prefer transparent systems (second trader can verify signal on chart); complex ones are hard to debug.
- Diagnosing "should work but doesn't" (pp.990-992): rule misread (volume > yesterday vs > 10d avg); wrong parameter range; one speed cannot capture variable holding periods (combine long-trend direction + medium-trend entries); similar markets should behave alike; ignoring a few big losses (LTCM); volatility growth (fixed size in Nasdaq 1996 vs 2000 = 6x risk); more participants = more noise -> trend systems lag more and decay.
- Extreme events (pp.992-1000): shocks cause the largest gap between test and live; diversification fails in shocks (money moves, not fundamentals). After a shock prices usually reverse over days; vol always rises. Crisis rule sketch: windfall profit -> exit; loss -> hold expecting reversal; re-add when vol falls. Trend lines go out of phase after a crash (1987: 50/100/200d MAs stayed short for months); use a defined crisis mode, re-initialise trends after vol subsides.
- Theory of runs (pp.1000-1011): P(run of n)=(1/2)^(n+2); longest run grows by 1 per doubling of trials. Martingale (double after loss) and anti-Martingale. Real markets: runs of 4-5 days slightly rarer than random; DAX has excess 1-day reversals (noisy), Eurodollar fewer. Martingale inside a 60d trend (add on adverse close up to 8x, strip back on favourable close) lifted info ratio on Eurodollar (6.67 -> 8.3) but not on S&P (1.18 -> 1.04 capped). Anti-Martingale cap 2: 10.95 on Eurodollar; uncapped loses. Only for strongly trending low-noise markets; not for us (blow-up risk).
- Gustafson mean-reversion (pp.1010-1011): long at next open after 4 consecutive lower closes; exit after 5 consecutive higher closes or after 8 days; S&P 1988-95: 78% wins, $35k, DD $5.8k; 1982-2001: 76% wins, $348k, DD $56k (long-only, bull market).
- Selective trading / manual overrides ruin consistency; no filter removes all losers; never mix in black boxes (pp.1011-1012).
- System trade-offs (pp.1012-1017): trend = many small losses, few large wins, 30-35% winners normal (smoothing systems 40-45% max); mean reversion = many small wins, few large losses, needs loose stops; can't have small losses AND high hit rate AND many opportunities. Mean-reversion entry distance: nearer = more trades but bigger stop needed; farther = few trades. Combine trend direction with support/resistance zones (no new shorts at support).
- Trading limits (pp.1018-1020): locked-limit days (O=H=L=C); irrelevant for FX/CFD except gap modelling.
- Silver/Nasdaq "too good to be true" (p.1020): "always buy" works only in the bull regime; only test is multi-regime profitability.
- Similarity of systems (pp.1021-1026): different trend systems correlated 0.4-0.8 monthly; same side of the market 55-83% of days; MA10 vs MA20 corr 0.51, MA50 vs MA60 0.75 (closer ratio = higher correlation). They are all in the same trade exactly when it pays and when a shock hits.

### Ch 23 - Risk Control (pp. 1027-1097)
- Mistaking luck for skill (p.1027-1028): shock windfalls must be viewed as potential losses; ask whether the account survives the same move the other way. Two rules: be in market as little as possible; earn as much as possible with as little invested (a 200%/yr fully-levered program is wiped out by a shock, 20% de-levered survives).
- Risk preference & efficient frontier (pp.1028-1032): choose the point on the frontier matching your tolerance.
- Common-sense rules (p.1032): risk a small % per trade (never >5%); exit conditions defined in advance; large profits imply large risk (reduce size); exit wrong trades quickly; don't meet margin calls; liquidate worst position first; stay consistent with philosophy; plan for contingencies.
- Liquidity/slippage (pp.1033-1034): execution cost grows with order size and volatility, shrinks with volume; for equal gross profit favour fewest trades in most liquid markets.
- Performance measures (pp.1034-1045): Sharpe = (E-I)/sigma; information ratio omits I. Sharpe cannot see ORDER of losses or tell upside surges from downside. Treynor (beta). Schwager's Average Maximum Retracement (mean drawdown from peak). Calmar = annual return / max drawdown. Sortino = (AROR-MAR)/downside deviation. Ulcer Index = root-mean-square drawdown. DP = stdev of drawdowns; SV = semivariance below regression line; drawdown ratio = AROR/DP. Monthly data looks better than daily. Higher risk if small sample, few drawdown episodes, few product groups, compounding. Annualised vol = stdev x sqrt(252). VaR variance-covariance and historical (apply today's positions to the last 252 days, mean - 2.33 sd for 99%); VaR fails when needed most (kurtosis up to 25 vs normal 3); use stress tests. Estimates from 100 samples carry ~10% error. 1%/month chance of 50% loss becomes ~50% over 50 months. Hindenburg Omen = NYSE breadth, not applicable.
- Trend vs breakout risk (p.1045): breakout trade risk at entry = highest high - lowest low of lookback; trend trade count cannot be cut below a floor even with very long lookbacks.
- Stability of returns (p.1046): slope through yearly returns reveals decay.
- Leverage & targeted risk (pp.1046-1050): size so that annualised stdev of daily P/L equals target vol: investment = annualised stdev of 1-unit P/L / target vol (15% typical; lowest practical 6-8%). 15% vol -> 2.5 sd loss ~37% of account, i.e. aggressive. Max-risk liquidation level usually 50%, as low as 10% (that would imply ~3% vol and 80% less return).
- Position sizing methods (pp.1050-1054): (1) equal dollar, (2) equal annualised-vol dollars, (3) equal ATR risk give wildly different sizes. Prefer vol/ATR-based; test across many markets on average information ratio. ATR sizing cut BAC trend P/L volatility ~27% vs fixed shares. Equal risk per trade = most conservative if trades cannot be ranked. With variable sizes, capital = largest amount ever needed.
- Stops (pp.1054-1062): for a random walk, hits x distance is constant, so a stop must beat chance; tie to volatility not fixed dollars. Types: parabolic, swing, N-bar extreme, KAMA, k x ATR. Initial stop: trend systems already cut losses; mean reversion needs room; test. Trailing: fixed %, volatility (ISAM suggests ~12 x ATR252), % of profits (after a trigger). Kase Dev-stop: ATR of 2-day true range (20-30 periods) + f x stdev, f=1, 2.06-2.25, 3.2-3.5, measured from trade extreme. Prefer close-based exits to dodge noise. 30y-bond test (MA 10-100 x stdev-stop factors 2-10): nearly all stops beat no-stop (ONE market/period). Stops fill at worst price in shocks; proximity risk when many systems cluster. Alternative: cut size when vol rises.
- Profit targets (pp.1057-1059): useful for short-term noisy trading, hurt slow trend following (80-bar MA best with no target; 5-bar MA on 60-min euro turned -3k to +19k at 7.5 x ATR20 but small and unstable across factors). Targets from vol multiples; scale out in 3 tranches.
- Risk overlays (p.1061): stop as % of margin (50-70%), % of account (1-2.5%), or max adverse excursion.
- Market ranking (pp.1062-1072): trend-quality metrics: r^2 over n days (>0.25), net move, regression slope, ADX >20, avg absolute change. Wilder CSI = ADXR x ATR14 x K. Chande trend strength = ln(P_t/P_t-n)/(stdev of 1-period log returns x sqrt n). Kaufman efficiency ratio ER = |net change over n| / sum of |daily changes| (65-day average ER 0.12-0.18). Higher average ER -> higher trend-system PF; indices (S&P ER 0.12, PF 1.16) and grains worst; short rates best. Markets with PF<1 suit mean reversion.
- Directional movement tests (pp.1067-1069): DM system over 4-80 days on 5 markets 2000-2011: best period different per market, high variation = not robust. Colby 2-day PDI/MDI/ADX rule on Dow 1928-2000 profitable (bull market).
- Risk of ruin (pp.1072-1076): equal win/loss: R=((1-A)/(1+A))^c, A=2p-1 (60% winners, 1 unit -> 33%, 2 units -> 11%). Unequal (Gehm/Vince): ruin = ((1-P)/P)^(MaxRisk/A), P=0.5(1+Z/A), Z=sum of prob-weighted win/loss as % of capital, A=sqrt(sum of squares). Worked: avg win 400, loss 200, 40% winners, $10k, 25% loss tolerance -> 0.63%; halve capital -> 7.9%; quarter -> 28%; avg win 325 -> 20%; with 15% tolerance -> 38%. Ruin grows faster than capital shrinks.
- Entry techniques (pp.1076-1080), 80-day MA on bonds/e-mini S&P/gold 1990-2011: (a) scale into 5 units spaced 1-5 days improved results (2-day spacing best; S&P from negative to positive); (b) wait up to 5 days for a threshold pullback else enter at close of day 5: trades -40%, profit per trade ~2x, S&P loss halved, gold loss -> profit; (c) wait for RSI(8)<30/>70 inside the window: helps but few signals. Window should scale with trend speed.
- Compounding (pp.1080-1084): adding equal units on NEW HIGH profits (spacing 1/3/5/10 days, max 5 units) raised profit factor in futures (e-mini S&P PF 1.14 -> 1.46 at 10 days) and was mixed on stocks. Averaging DOWN was worse in 3 of 4 markets. Compounded positions are top-heavy.
- Equity trends (pp.1085-1088): compounding vs fixed-size illustrated with +100%/-50% cycles. Trading the equity curve (exit when below its 10d/60d MA) was inconsistent (helped S&P, gold; hurt bonds; no switching costs). Test autocorrelation of returns first; hypothetical equity behaves worse than real.
- Optimal f / Kelly (pp.1088-1092): Kelly G(f)=p ln(1+Bf)+(1-p)ln(1-f); Vince optimal f maximises geometric mean over f=0.01..1.0 (fraction of largest loss); closed form f=[p(PLR+1)-1]/PLR (p=0.5, PLR=2 -> 25%). Elder's caveats: moves with every trade, over-betting leads to ruin, under-betting gives up geometrically, uses closed-trade results (hides intratrade drawdown). Markowitz approximation: optimal leverage = mean/variance. Kaufman: use only to REDUCE leverage.
- Expected vs actual (pp.1092-1097): binomial loss-count table for a 1/3-win system (15 trades: 13+ losses ~8%; >12 suspicious). Chi-square on win rate: observed 20% vs expected 35% gives chi2 9.89 (significant; df=1 thresholds 3.84 / 6.64 / 10.83 for 5% / 1% / 0.1%). Chi-square on run-length counts vs random: 5.47, not significant. Yates correction for small counts. Need enough data (error ~1/sqrt N).

### Ch 24 - Diversification and Portfolio Allocation (pp. 1099-1145)
- Diversify across market groups with little relation; within financials (rates, FX, indices) news creates predictable joint moves. Ideal: risk falls like 1/n (2 assets 50%, 4 assets 25%); in reality a full portfolio might cut risk ~50%; gain flattens after ~4 assets (Fig 24.1).
- Diversify across STRATEGY TYPES (trend, countertrend, spreads, fundamental, carry). All trend followers (MA, P&F, breakout) share the same profit source.
- Balanced risk: equal dollar vs equal stdev vs equal ATR. Size inversely to annualised stdev (200d), normalise weights (BAC/MSFT/AAPL example).
- Negatively correlated zero-return system can cut risk by more than half; leverage back up (return ratio improves).
- Changing correlations (pp.1105-1106): 60-day rolling correlation of S&P vs T-notes, euro, gold swings strongly; in the 2008 crash euro/gold went to -0.6 vs S&P in weeks; shorter windows more extreme. Defences: be out of market as much as possible, cap portfolio leverage, use uncorrelated strategies.
- Portfolio models (Narang): equal-dollar, equal-risk, alpha-driven (rank not scale), decision-tree, mean-variance.
- Classic MPT: E(R)=sum w E(R_i), variance with covariances; also check max portfolio loss and count VaR spikes (many = too much leverage). Excel Solver recipe: align returns (carry forward missing days), start equal weights, maximise return/vol with weights >=0 and sum 1. Example improved ratio 1.37 -> 1.97 in-sample (hindsight). Re-estimation windows (3-5y refit monthly/quarterly) have no proven best.
- GASP (genetic algorithm portfolio, pp.1114-1141): mean-variance fails for TRADING returns: a strategy flat half the time has artificially low stdev and gets over-allocated; covariance is dominated by days only one strategy trades; selective strategies with high return per active day get eliminated. Fix: risk = stdev of drawdowns (semivariance), objective = AROR/D, constraints: liquidity (<=1-3% of daily volume), no shorts, risk band, optimal subset s of S. GA: pool 5000, 100 cycles x 3-5 restarts (5x50-100 better than 1x500), propagation, mating 10-20%, mutation 5-15%. Example: 19 trend/mean-reversion long/short Nasdaq strategies, 7.16y, subset 12; objective 12.96 -> 17.9, allocations differ per pass but structure similar. Idea transferable: allocate on the R trade stream by drawdown-based ratio.
- Test interval (pp.1141-1142): long history with bull and bear and full seasonal cycles; rolling 6-month optimisations too short.
- Volatility stabilisation (pp.1142-1145): scale positions by target vol / lagged rolling realised vol (10-day example; 12% target gave 13.7% realised). Info ratio 1.27 -> 1.38 over 17y. Switching costs: longer vol window and/or rebalance only if factor moves >20%. Cap exposure last (UCITS 2.5x equity); if exposure > cap, scale ALL positions by cap/exposure; reduces returns but barely info ratio.

## Topic tags
- trend: slower lookbacks usually better/more robust; market trendiness matters more than method; all trend systems fail in noise (indices, grains); pullback-in-trend (fade fast inside slow, p.950) and delayed entry (p.1078); rank markets by efficiency ratio.
- breakout: N-day breakout reliable but initial risk = lookback range; ~2.7 trades/yr on 20-80 days.
- mean-reversion: Gustafson 4-down-closes long; mean reversion suits markets where trend PF<1; needs loose stops/high hit rate; entry-distance trade-off.
- momentum/oscillators: RSI(8) 30/70 as entry timing inside a trend mildly helpful; ADX/DM rules not robust.
- patterns: none; runs of 4-5 days rarer than random.
- intraday/sessions: nothing; costs hurt fast systems; intraday needs profit targets.
- seasonality/time: allocation windows must contain full seasonal cycle.
- volatility: size and stops from ATR/stdev; vol targeting stabilises and raises info ratio; filters must beat the plain core on % profitable combos.
- stops-and-exits: stops volatility-based, on close; trailing > initial; fill risk in shocks; profit targets hurt trend following; scale out; stop-and-reverse avoided (separate entries/exits cut slippage).
- position-sizing/risk: ATR sizing; target vol 6-15%; <=1-5% per trade; risk-of-ruin formulas; Kelly/optimal f only to reduce leverage; never average down; add only on new-high profits; capital ~3x max DD.
- testing-and-robustness/overfitting: see checklist. Numbers: 50% data OOS; OOS ~50% of IS normal; >70% of tests in range profitable; <=4 parameters; retest after 5-10% new data; judge by average not best.
- costs/execution: test with 0 / realistic / stressed costs; paper trade with worst-of-5-min fill; monitor live slippage.
- psychology/discipline: do not override; no black boxes; fix expectations first.
- portfolio/diversification: uncorrelated markets AND strategy types; correlations -> 1 in shocks; equal-risk weights; drawdown-based objective; vol stabilisation; leverage cap.

## Testable ideas
(Mostly entry/management/sizing layers; Kaufman gives no new alpha in these chapters.)

1. ★ **Pullback entry inside a slow trend (fade the fast trend)** - Entry long: slow trend up (e.g. price above/slope of SMA 100-200 or KAMA) AND fast trend (10-20 bar MA) turns down; short mirrored. Exit when slow trend flips (or ATR trail); stop k x ATR (3x as in strategy 4). 1h-1d bars. - Ch21 pp.950-951 - Claimed Nasdaq 1991-2011: PF 1.51 -> 2.26, win 35% -> 47%, profit/trade 277 -> 741, trades -66%, net slightly lower. - Fit: reasonable; fewer trades and less exposure means less swap; test on the same trend baselines as strategy 4. - Differs: no stop-and-reverse, entry on counter-move, holds only while slow trend intact.

2. ★ **Delayed / better-price entry window after a trend signal** - After trend-change signal wait up to N bars (book: 5 days on 80d MA; scale N with trend speed) for a pullback of a minimum threshold (fraction of ATR) from the signal price; if none, enter at close of bar N; cancel if trend flips back. - Ch23 pp.1078-1079, Table 23.10 - Claimed: trades -40%, profit/trade ~2x, S&P loss halved, gold loss -> profit (1990-2011). - Fit: good; skips whipsaws, lowers swap/spread drag. - Differs: entry timing layer on any signal (strategy 4 entered immediately).

3. ★ **Scale in over several bars** - Enter 1/5 of the position at signal, rest every ~2 bars; position weights R by average entry; if trend flips mid-fill only a partial loss. - Ch23 pp.1076-1077, Table 23.9 - Claimed: best spacing 2 bars on 80d MA, turned S&P from negative to positive; bonds/gold improved. - Fit: easy in R (average entry price), but tranche costs scale (check spread). - Differs: execution change only.

4. ★ **Volatility-targeted sizing and stabilisation (risk module)** - Risk per trade = r% / (k x ATR); portfolio scale = target vol / lagged rolling realised vol (10-20 day) of strategy P/L; only rebalance if factor moves >20%. Apply on top of R list; test FTMO 5%/10% breach probability. - Ch23 pp.1048-1054, Ch24 pp.1142-1145 - Claimed: info ratio 1.27 -> 1.38 (17y), position vol -27% on one stock. - Fit: excellent fit with risk/ layer. - Differs: sizing, not signal.

5. **Efficiency-ratio regime/market filter** - ER_n = |C_t - C_(t-n)| / sum|C_i - C_(i-1)|; run trend rules only when/where ER high, mean-reversion when low; pre-declare n (65 on daily). - Ch23 pp.1070-1072 - Claimed: PF vs ER rises across 26 markets; indices (ER .12) worst. - Fit: moderate; NAS100/US500 are the noisy class, FX/gold may pass; intraday ER untested by author. - Differs: regime selector. Snooping risk on threshold.

6. **Add on new-high profits (pyramiding)** - After entry add one unit each time the trade makes a new profit high, at least n bars apart (1/3/5/10), max 5 units; never add on losses. - Ch23 pp.1082-1084, Table 23.12 - Claimed: PF up in futures (e-mini S&P 1.14 -> 1.46 at 10 days), mixed on stocks. - Fit: ok if combined risk capped for FTMO; test in R with combined stop. - Differs: management layer.

7. **Gustafson 4-down-closes long** - Long at next open after 4 consecutive lower closes; exit next open after 5 consecutive higher closes or after 8 bars; no stop in book (add ATR stop). - Ch22 pp.1010-1011 - Claimed: S&P 1982-2001 76% wins, $348k vs DD $56k. - Fit: moderate-poor (bull-market long bias, overnight swap on CFDs for 1-8 days); cheap daily/1h test on NAS100/US500. - Differs: short-term mean reversion, not trend/session/SMC.

8. **Shock-day rule** - Shock bar = range >3.5-5 x ATR(10): profit -> exit; loss -> hold up to N bars or block new entries for N bars; re-arm when vol falls. - Ch22 pp.997-1000, Ch21 pp.970-972 - Claimed: price reverses after shock in all four case studies. - Fit: good as FTMO daily-loss protection around NFP/FOMC on XAUUSD/NAS100; few events -> thin stats. - Differs: exit/risk overlay.

9. **Equity-curve filter (trade only when strategy equity above its 10d/60d MA)** - Ch23 p.1086 - Author found inconsistent results (hurt bonds). Poor fit; test only if cheap.

## Checklist to apply to every backtest (derived from Ch21-24)
**Before running**
1. Write the premise (why the edge should exist: behaviour, flow, structure) and the expected trade count, win rate, avg R, holding time, drawdown. Compare actual with expectation; investigate surprises in both directions.
2. Pre-register parameter ranges (sensible, geometric spacing), number of free parameters (aim <=4) and the success metric (risk-adjusted: net expectancy in R, ratio on daily returns, Calmar/Ulcer, not net profit alone). Keep a global counter of every variant ever tried.
3. Fix data splits first: reserve ~50% out-of-sample (random alternating blocks decided in advance, e.g. by year/quarter) plus sibling symbols as free OOS (GBPUSD vs GBPJPY, NAS100 vs US500). OOS touched once; any change afterwards = new strategy, new split.
4. Verify rules by hand on a sample of trades (chart check); no lookahead; data cleaning (spikes, gaps, bad OHLC, DST edges).
5. Costs on from the start; run 3 cost levels (0, realistic spread+commission+swap, +50% stress) and show how the profitable parameter range shrinks.

**Parameter surface and robustness**
6. Test one parameter at a time, core signal first, stops/filters later; core must be profitable without stop fixes.
7. Show 1-D curve / 2-D heat map; accept only broad plateaus; reject lone spikes and erratic maps. Smooth with 3-point / 9-cell neighbour average before choosing; pick plateau centre, not the max.
8. Report the AVERAGE over the whole pre-declared range (net R, trades, max DD, expectancy) with its st.dev; adjusted expectation = mean - 1 sd. Robustness = share of combos profitable (target >70%, minimum 50%).
9. Sensitivity: perturb chosen params +-10-20% and in n-space; degradation should be gentle.
10. A rule/filter must improve the average over all tests (or cut dispersion at same mean); if it only lifts the peak or touches 1-2 trades, drop it. Compare % profitable combos with vs without filter.
11. Cross-market and cross-timeframe: same rules and parameters on all symbols of a class and neighbouring timeframes; profitable in >=50% of markets; one-symbol-only = overfit.
12. Walk-forward: windows long enough that the best parameter does not flip between fast and slow; chain OOS pieces only; report parameter stability. Keep a final hold-out.
13. Time stability: yearly/half-year P/L table and slope of periodic returns; reject decay or profit from 1-2 episodes. Remove the best single trade/move; study the 10 biggest wins and losses.
14. Shock check: mark shock days (range >3.5-5 x ATR10); report P/L with shock-day sign flipped or removed; >50% profitable shocks means understated risk. Report largest single-bar loss vs stop distance (gap risk).
15. Trade count: dozens to hundreds; report standard error of expectancy; ignore profit factor under ~30 trades. Context: trend 30-40% wins normal; mean reversion needs >60%.
16. Report time in market and swap cost in R separately; prefer exits to reversals.

**Risk and sizing (R list -> account)**
17. Convert R trades to account results with fixed fraction (0.25-1% per trade; Kaufman says never >5%) and also vol-targeted sizing; for drawdown Monte Carlo use block bootstrap plus the real sequence (he warns plain reshuffling is harsher than reality for trend systems).
18. Treat historical max DD as a minimum; plan for ~1.5-2x; capital >= ~3x max DD. For FTMO: size so P(5% daily or 10% total breach) is under a stated threshold by simulation.
19. Compute risk of ruin vs the FTMO limits from win rate, avg win/loss, risk per trade (pp.1073-1075); check that ruin rises faster than capital falls.
20. Kelly/optimal f only as an upper bound; trade a fraction.
21. Live monitoring: binomial probability of observed loss streak (p.1093) and chi-square of live vs expected win rate (p.1094) to decide if a system is "broken".
22. Drawdown metrics beyond Sharpe: Calmar, Ulcer Index, average maximum retracement, time to recover.
23. Portfolio: equal R risk per symbol (ATR), daily correlation of strategy R streams (also in stress windows), cap simultaneous open risk; correlated trend strategies don't diversify.
24. Vol-stabilise / cap total exposure; avoid resizing on small vol changes (>20% threshold, longer window).

**Before live**
25. Paper/forward test with worst-of-5-minutes fills, same rules and same data source; compare to expectation; start small; retest only after 5-10% new data and expect small parameter shifts.

## Warnings / pitfalls (Kaufman)
- Optimisation is the most misused tool; the best result never lives up in trading; using OOS more than once or fixing a rule after seeing OOS destroys it.
- Profit factor/net profit with tiny trade counts or closed-trade-only accounting mislead; surprisingly small max DD means overfit.
- A system that profited from more than half of historical shocks has understated risk; backtests treat shocks as normal days and chosen parameters are their biggest beneficiaries (why live < backtest).
- Rule changes made to fix a specific bad stretch are overfitting; resist retuning after losses.
- Equal-increment grids bias averages toward slow settings; coded (rule-switch) parameters do not form surfaces.
- Costs: fast systems lose to slippage; with zero costs many fast systems look great.
- Data-source mismatch between test and trade; back-adjusted/synthetic data hide roll cost (not an issue for spot CFD, but feed differences vs FTMO are).
- Diversification disappears in crises (correlations -> 1); VaR/correlation stats fail exactly then; use stress tests.
- Martingale/pyramiding/optimal-f/high leverage turn small edges into ruin when a shock arrives; never average down or meet margin calls.
- Trend systems are mutually correlated, lose in the same noisy markets (indices), 30-40% win rate and long flat spells; single-market tests mislead.
- Benchmarks have survivorship bias; compare to something simple.
- Historic max drawdown is always understated; real results are worse than hypothetical.
- Equity-curve trading and short-window re-optimisation have no robust evidence.
