### Overview
- Covers: ch.8 sections 8.1-8.3 (expectations, convexity, forward-rate decomposition, risk premium; TK pp.197-204), ch.10 repo and financing (pp.223-247), ch.11 Treasury note and bond futures (pp.249-287).
- Pure mechanics and no-arbitrage pricing book. No backtests and no return statistics. The author's stance: the repo market and futures contract design explain relative prices; excess returns are mostly compensation for funding, liquidity and optionality risk, not free lunches.
- Most useful for us: (a) the cost-of-carry identity for bond futures (forward drop = coupon minus repo), (b) why a long bond futures position earns the term premium only as duration risk premium, (c) how the contract is built (CTD, conversion factors, delivery options), (d) when funding stress appears in repo (quarter end, year end, Treasury settlement days), (e) the basis-trade case study showing who is forced to trade in stress.
- Nothing in the text is specific to month-end index fund duration extension. It gives the vocabulary and the plumbing to judge I-109 (long 10-year futures, last 3 trade dates of a month) and its cost and carry accounting.

### Chapter notes

### Ch 8 - Expectations, Risk Premium, Convexity (pp.197-204; assigned part only)
- With no rate uncertainty the forward curve equals expected short rates (p.197). Near-term expectations can be granular (central bank meeting dates, tax dates, issuance calendar, quarterly balance sheet management), long-term ones are smooth (p.198). So calendar-driven short-rate effects exist, but only near-term.
- Convexity example (pp.198-200): flat expected short rate of 9% with 4% rate volatility gives a downward forward curve (8.85%, 8.56%). Reason: Jensen's inequality, expected price of a bond exceeds the price at the expected rate.
- Instantaneous return of a zero (eq. 8.15, p.202) = forward rate x dt (carry/roll) minus duration x rate change plus 0.5 x convexity x variance x dt. Three components: time passage, duration times rate move, convexity gain.
- Risk premium: risk-averse investors demand expected excess return proportional to duration (eq. 8.18, p.203). Example: 1% short rate, duration 5, premium 10 bp per year of duration gives 1.5% expected return. Bond Sharpe ratio = premium / rate volatility (example: 10 bp / 100 bp = 0.10; p.203).
- Forward rate = short rate + (expected rate change x duration) + (risk premium x duration) - 0.5 x convexity x variance (eq. 8.20, p.203). Key point: expectations and risk premium cannot be separated from the cross-section of bond prices (p.204). A drift in expected rates and a risk premium are observationally the same.
- Pure expectations hypothesis (premium = 0): "no-change" means forward rates are realized. Pure risk-premium hypothesis (no expected change): "no-change" means an unchanged curve is realized. Reality is a mix (p.204).
- Convexity is not free: the lower forward rate it causes is exactly offset by the higher realized convexity return, so no net advantage (p.204).

### Ch 10 - Repurchase Agreements and Financing (pp.223-247)
- 10.1 (pp.223-225): repo = secured loan, legally a sale plus repurchase, so it has bankruptcy safe harbor. Protection: haircut (2% Treasuries typical, p.226/234), daily variation margin. Example: 100mm face loan at 0.015% for 44 days, interest = principal x rate x days/360.
- 10.2 (pp.226-230): four uses: cash investing (money funds, municipalities, corporates park cash), long financing (dealers repo out inventory; only the overnight residual position needs funding), short financing (reverse repo to borrow a specific bond), collateral swaps. Repo can be overnight, term, or open. Repos are rolled, so there is rollover risk.
- General collateral (GC) versus special: GC lender accepts any Treasury, special repo requires one specific bond (p.230).
- 10.3 (pp.231-234): US repo market structure, tri-party (~$3.0 trillion collateral, 62% Treasuries), GCF (~$238bn), bilateral DVP (~$1.2 trillion), July 2021. Median haircuts 2% (Treasuries, agency) up to 8% (equities, high yield). Only dealers, money funds, and a few sectors matter; money funds are the main cash lenders.
- 10.4 (pp.234-235): SOFR = volume-weighted median of tri-party and trimmed DVP repo, bottom 25% of DVP volume excluded to remove specials. Example day 14 May 2021: median 1 bp, 1st to 99th percentile -4 to +15 bp, volume $865bn.
- 10.5 GC and specials (pp.235-241):
  - EFFR minus GC spread: median about +1 bp, 5th to 95th percentile -13 to +5 bp (as printed), 0.5th to 99.5th percentile -37 to +113 bp, Jan 2001 to Jul 2021 (Table 10.5, p.236).
  - Spikes up in GC rates (cash scarcity): Sept 2001, 30 Sept 2016 (quarter end), 31 Dec 2018 (year end), Sept 2019 (tax payments plus new Treasury settlements plus a Japanese holiday), March 2020. The author says quarter and year end "window dressing" pushes repo rates up, and that the Fed has made such disruptions rarer but not extinct (p.236).
  - Spikes down in GC rates: Aug 2007, Sept 2008 (flight to safe assets).
  - Special spread = GC minus special rate. On-the-run 10-year specials in May 2021 ranged from a few bp to over 10 bp (Table 10.6, damaged numbers). Notes get special around the first reopening of a new issue (auction cycle: new 10-year in Feb, May, Aug, Nov; reopening after one and two months).
  - Average 10-year special spread over the auction cycle, 51 notes, Nov 2008 to Jul 2021 (Fig 10.10, p.240): low just after auction, rising into the next auction, peaking at about 156 bp the day before first reopening (2 sd range 116-196 bp), about 48 bp before second reopening, under 20 bp before the next new issue. Explanation: liquidity shifts to the new on-the-run, dealers short it to hedge their purchases from the coming auction.
  - Since 2009 a fail-to-deliver penalty is max(0, 3% minus fed funds), so specials can go to negative repo rates (footnote 8, p.239).
- 10.6 (pp.241-243): repo is cheap but unstable funding. Post-2008 liquidity rules (LCR, NSFR) and the supplementary leverage ratio discourage banks from lending reserves into Treasury repo; the author links this to the Sept 2019 and March 2020 rate spikes when banks did not lend despite high rates. Regulators exempted Treasuries and reserves from the SLR from 15 May 2020 to 31 Mar 2021.
- 10.7 MF Global (pp.243-247): repo-to-maturity on weak European sovereign bonds. Haircuts raised from 3% to up to 80% (Ireland, Portugal) by July 2011; margin on a ~$6bn book rose from $170mm to $665mm in four and a half months; loss over $400mm on liquidation although 50% of the book matured within two months. Lesson: size the position for the worst funding and margin change, not the expected carry.

### Ch 11 - Note and Bond Futures (pp.249-287)
- Intro (pp.249-250): the 10-year note future has the largest share of traded DV01 of all US Treasury instruments (Fig 11.1, 2017-18 sample). Futures are used because of liquidity and low cash needs. The US contracts embed quality and timing options; European and Japanese ones have no timing option.
- 11.1 Forward prices (pp.250-256): forward price follows from buying the bond and repo-financing to the forward date. Full forward price = full spot price x (1 + repo x days/360) minus the future value of intermediate coupons. Forward bond prices are normally below spot ("forward drop"), and the drop is approximately carry = coupon interest earned minus repo cost over the period. Example: 2.875s of 2028, 136 days, drop about 1.06 (spot 110.773 vs forward 109.717). Carry is positive when the curve is upward sloping (coupon above repo rate); negative carry reverses the sign.
- 11.2-11.3 (pp.256-258): forward yield is above spot yield when repo is below the yield (spot yield is a weighted average of repo and forward yield). DV01 of a forward vs spot yield, forward yield, and repo rate: the position is a long spot bond plus a short repo; the repo-rate DV01 is small and negative. Hedge repo-rate risk with short-term instruments and yield risk with long ones (two-factor view).
- 11.4 Mechanics (pp.258-261): the 10-year contract (TY) is $100,000 face notional, price in 32nds (plus half ticks), deliverable basket of notes with remaining maturity roughly 6.5-10 years (basket in Table 11.5 for TYU1: 14 notes from 2028 to Feb 2031), conversion factors (CF) 0.64-0.84. Seller picks which note (quality option) and when in the delivery month (timing option); two more options: end-of-month and wild card. Daily settlement: 1 tick (1/32 per 100 face) = $31.25 per contract; example daily settlement table for TYU1 (Table 11.6), daily changes of a few ticks up to 18 ticks. Exchange maintenance margin per contract (e.g. $1,275).
- 11.5 Daily settlement (pp.261-263): futures price is slightly below the forward price, because a long bond futures holder receives gains when rates are low (poor reinvestment) and pays losses when rates are high (expensive funding); the difference is small for note futures (short expiry relative to bond maturity, low covariance). Hedges are tailed: N_fut = N_fwd / (1 + r d/360). Terminology: mark-to-market (accounting, no cash), variation margin (collateral, returned and earns interest), daily settlement (irrevocable payment).
- 11.6-11.8 Cost of delivery and CTD (pp.263-269): cost of delivery = bond flat price minus CF x futures price. The cheapest-to-deliver (CTD) bond has the minimum price/CF ratio; at expiration futures price = CTD price / CF (Table 11.7: 2.875s of 05/2028 CTD at a cost of 0.005). Conversion factor is the price at a 6% notional coupon, so with a flat 6% curve every bond is equally deliverable. When yields are below 6% the shortest-duration bond is CTD (low yield regime, as in 2021 where the curve was far below 6%); at high yields the longest-duration bond is CTD. Flatter curve favors shorter bonds, steeper curve favors longer ones. Basket and CF reduce squeeze risk and keep liquidity from depending on one bond.
- 11.9 Gross and net basis (pp.269-273): gross basis = bond price - CF x futures; net basis = forward price - CF x futures = gross basis minus carry (Table 11.8, 14 May 2021: CTD gross basis 34.9 ticks, carry 33.8, net basis 1.1 ticks). Basis trade = buy bond forward (or via repo), sell CF-weighted tailed number of futures; P&L = face x change in net basis. Net basis is zero for the CTD at expiration.
- 11.10 Implied repo (pp.273-274): the repo rate implied by buying the CTD spot and selling it through futures. Implied repo above actual repo means futures rich; below means cheap. Example: implied -0.065% vs actual 0.015% means TYU1 cheap versus the 2028 note.
- 11.11 Quality option before expiry (pp.274-279): futures price = forward of CTD / CF minus the value of the seller's switching option. The option is worth most when yields are near the CTD switch point and volatility is high; the futures contract is negatively convex in that region. In the 2021 low-yield regime the option was worth little and the future behaved like a forward on one bond. Notional coupon kept at 6% (lowered from 8% earlier) because liquidity is high and a simpler contract is preferred.
- 11.12 Timing, end-of-month and wild-card options (pp.279-281): carry positive means deliver late. After the last trading date (7 business days before the last delivery date) the futures price freezes; hedges must then be re-set from CF-weighted to matched notional. Wild-card: settlement at 2 p.m. CT, delivery notice a few hours later, so intraday price moves after settlement can be exploited by shorts. These are options for sellers (shorts), so a long carries them as a cost.
- 11.13 March 2020 case (pp.281-287): futures traded rich to bonds for years (TYM0 net basis between 0 and -5 ticks in Feb 2020) because regulated institutions prefer off-balance-sheet futures to cash bonds. Hedge funds bought the basis at -4 ticks, levered 20-40x, about 6.5%-13% annualized. In March 2020 the net basis hit -18.5 ticks (loss of $3.2mm on $1bn in one day vs 2.5mm target profit), exchange margin rose from $1,275 to $1,600 per contract, and rolling overnight repo was risky (99th percentile repo rate hit 174 bp above SOFR on 16 March). Spread between CTD yields and non-deliverable comparable bonds rose to 10-18 bp (Fig 11.12), futures-eligible bonds trade at a liquidity premium in stress.

### Topic tags
- seasonality/time: repo specials follow the Treasury auction cycle (peak around the first reopening, about 156 bp average the day before, TK p.240); GC repo spikes at quarter end, year end, tax dates and new Treasury settlement days (p.236, p.237); the futures contract has fixed calendar mechanics: last trading date 7 business days before last delivery date (p.279).
- costs/execution: carry (coupon minus repo) is the cost or yield of holding the bond over the period; futures price is slightly below the forward price because of daily settlement (p.262); basis trades need tailing (p.263); repo haircuts and margin changes are the real financing cost in stress (pp.245-246, 285-286).
- volatility: delivery options are worth most when volatility is high and the curve is near the CTD switch point (pp.275-278).
- position-sizing/risk: MF Global and March 2020 basis trades show that margin and haircut changes force exits well before the trade converges (pp.243-247, 285-286); size for worst-case margin.
- portfolio/diversification: the term premium is compensation per unit of duration risk (Sharpe-like ratio premium / rate volatility, p.203); the cross-section of bonds cannot separate expectations from risk premium (p.204).
- testing-and-robustness/overfitting: the cross-section identity forward = expected + premium - convexity warns that a forward-rate "mispricing" is not evidence of a tradable edge without separate evidence of expectations (p.204).
- trend, breakout, mean-reversion, momentum/oscillators, patterns, intraday/sessions, stops-and-exits, psychology/discipline: nothing in this part.

### Testable ideas

1. ★ **Treasury futures carry and roll-down regression for I-109** - Compute, for each month-end window, the carry of the 10-year contract (CTD coupon yield minus repo rate, scaled by days held, about 3 trading days) and add it as a known cost or benefit in the I-109 expected return. Rule: expected return over the window = carry for those days + duration x (expected yield change). Test whether the realized I-109 average return is explained by carry alone (null) or exceeds it. Source: ch.11.1, eq. 11.1, pp.252-256. Claim: forward drop equals carry. Fit: good (we have Yahoo continuous 10-year futures and FRED yields, can proxy repo by SOFR or T-bill). Difference from failed families: not a price pattern, a mechanical accounting check that separates the month-end flow effect from ordinary term premium and carry.

2. ★ **Month-end repo and funding stress calendar overlay** - Using EFFR/SOFR-GC style spreads (FRED SOFR and EFFR if available) test whether funding stress days (calendar month-end, quarter-end, mid-month Treasury settlement dates around the 15th) coincide with the days where the I-109 window earns more or less. Rule: split I-109 returns by quarter-end vs ordinary month-end (and by month in the Treasury refunding cycle: Feb/May/Aug/Nov). Parameters: 3 trade dates before the end of the month (as in I-109). Source: ch.10.5, pp.235-237 (quarter end, year end, Sept 2019 spikes). Claim: GC rates spike at quarter and year ends. Fit: good (FRED SOFR from 2018 only, EFFR longer; only ~8 years of SOFR). Differs from the spent month-end index flow test by looking at the funding side and at the interaction with the Treasury refunding calendar.

3. ★ **Refunding-cycle and auction-calendar seasonality of 10-year futures** - Test 10-year (or ZN continuous) returns around Treasury auction settlement dates and refunding months (15 Feb, May, Aug, Nov; 10-year auctions in the second week of Feb/May/Aug/Nov with reopenings in the other months). Rule: long the 10-year future from the close before the auction to the settlement date, or short, versus a matched non-auction control window. Source: ch.10.5 auction cycle (Fig 10.10, p.240) and ch.8 on issuance calendar (p.198). Claim: specialness peaks into first reopening because dealers short the on-the-run to hedge auction purchases; the book gives no price-return evidence, only the repo side. Fit: medium (continuous futures and FRED yields suffice; calendar of auction dates must be built; sample ~20 years). Differs from failed families: counterparty must trade (dealers hedge, Treasury supplies), not a pattern.

4. ★ **Futures-versus-cash richness (implied repo minus SOFR) as a regime filter** - The text says futures traded rich to bonds for years because of balance sheet preferences (pp.281-282), and cheap/rich shows up in implied repo vs actual repo. Without per-contract data this cannot be built directly; a proxy is the spread between the Yahoo continuous 10-year future yield-equivalent and the FRED 10-year constant-maturity yield. Rule: test whether the proxy spread's z-score predicts the I-109 window return or general 10-year future returns. Source: ch.11.10 (p.273), ch.11.13 (p.281). Fit: poor to medium (no CTD identification, no contract-level history, proxy is noisy). Parked unless contract-level data appears.

5. **Short-term carry-sign filter on I-109** - Only hold the month-end long when carry is positive (curve upward sloping between 2-year or T-bill and the 10-year; coupon above repo). Rule: carry = (10y yield - repo proxy) x days / 360; enter only if carry > 0. Source: ch.11.1 (p.255) and 11.12 (p.279): negative carry flips the early/late delivery logic. Fit: good as a conditioning test with the FRED curve. Risk: it reduces sample size on a window already close to the power limit; pre-register and count as one extra trial.

6. **Duration-extension intensity scaling** - Scale the position size with expected duration (recent 10-year duration changes with the yield level, ~8 years at 1.5% yields versus ~7 at 4%): the book's decomposition says the return premium scales with duration, so the same flow produces a bigger yield change per dollar when duration is high. Rule: position size inversely proportional to futures DV01 so the risk is constant. Source: ch.8 eq. 8.15 and 8.18, pp.202-203. Fit: good (DV01 can be approximated from yield level). A risk-normalizing change, not a new alpha; use only for the final portfolio sizing.

7. **Micro 10-Year Yield futures cash settlement check** - The CME micro yield futures are cash settled on the yield, so the contract has no CTD, no quality option and no delivery option (the issues in 11.6-11.12 do not apply). Use: the same flow hypothesis tested on yield changes rather than prices; carry is not embedded in yield futures the same way, so test the average yield change over the window with no carry correction. Source: contrast with ch.11.4-11.8 (pp.258-269); the book itself does not cover yield futures. Fit: good for the live venue (and it avoids price-based DV01 drift), but only the claim "no delivery options" is from the book; the contract's own spec must be verified at CME before relying on it.

8. **Funding-stress day exclusion for risk** - Reduce or skip the long when repo funding stress flags (SOFR minus EFFR spread large, or SOFR above its 20-day median by more than X bp). Source: ch.10.5, pp.236-237. Fit: medium (SOFR only from 2018). Use only as a risk rule, since basis-trade style forced selling in stress (March 2020) can hit a long position too.

### Warnings / pitfalls
- The term structure alone cannot separate expectations from risk premium (p.204); a "mispriced forward" cannot be turned into a trade without an independent estimate of expected rates.
- The futures price is a forward price minus the value of the seller's delivery options plus a small daily settlement adjustment; the continuous series of a rolled future mixes contract months with different CTDs and roll carry, so returns in a continuous-futures backtest include roll effects (not stated this way in the book, our inference from pp.263-281).
- The CTD can change with yield level and curve shape (pp.268-269, 275-278); negative convexity of the futures price near a switch point (p.277) makes a constant duration assumption wrong at times. In the 2021 low-yield regime the option was nearly worthless, but this regime dependence is a risk in other years.
- Basis trades looked riskless but were forced out by margin and haircut increases, loss limits and overnight repo risk (pp.285-287); the same flow-driven reasoning means a crowded month-end trade can reverse sharply in stress.
- Quarter-end and year-end funding spikes are real but the Fed has damped them over time (p.236), so the effect size in older samples may overstate the current effect.
- Repo data in our dataset is thin (no per-collateral repo history, SOFR only from 2018, EFFR as a proxy for older years); implied repo and net basis cannot be computed without contract-level futures and bond prices.
- This book gives no empirical return statistics for any of these effects; all numbers above (except the author's own cited averages for specials and basis) are mechanics, not evidence of tradable edges.
